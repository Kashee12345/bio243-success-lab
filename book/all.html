Chapter 1

Welcome to A&P

Why it feels different, and why you can do it

Anatomy and Physiology I is one of the most important courses you will take on the way to nursing or a health career, and for many students it is also the first course that feels truly hard. This chapter explains why it feels that way, shows you how BIO 243 is built, and makes one promise: the struggle you may feel early on is normal, and it can be beaten with the right system.

Why A&P feels different

Most students walk into BIO 243 having done well in high school science or in an earlier college class. Then, somewhere in the first few weeks, something changes. The reading takes longer than expected. The lecture moves fast. A quiz question that looked easy turns out to be asking something you did not see coming. If that happens to you, it does not mean you are in the wrong place. It means you have met a course that works differently from most of the classes you have taken before.

There are four main reasons A&P feels different. Once you can name them, you can plan for them.

1. The vocabulary load is huge

People often say that A&P is like learning a new language, and that comparison is fair. Each chapter of the OpenStax textbook ends with a list of key terms, and many chapters list dozens of them. Across the chapters in BIO 243, that adds up to many hundreds of new words. Some of them look alike (osteoblast, osteoclast, osteocyte). Some of them sound alike (ileum and ilium are not even in the same body system). And you are expected to know what each one means, where it is, and what it does.

The good news is that this language has rules. Most terms are built from Greek and Latin word parts. Once you know that osteo- means bone, -blast means a builder, and -clast means a breaker, the words osteoblast and osteoclast start to explain themselves. Chapter 3 of this book shows you how to build a running vocabulary list with word roots, so the language gets easier every week instead of harder.

2. The pace is fast

BIO 243 covers a lot of ground in one semester. In a typical term you move from body organization and chemistry to cells, tissues, skin, bone, joints, muscle and the whole nervous system. A new chapter can begin before you feel finished with the last one. That is why the syllabus asks you to read before class and to study steadily every week. In A&P, falling a week behind is easy, and catching up is hard.

3. You learn three things at once

For almost every structure, you need to know its name, its structure (what it looks like and where it is), and its function (what it does and why). Physiology adds a fourth layer: processes. A process is a chain of events, such as how a muscle fiber contracts or how a nerve signal travels. Knowing the names of the parts is not enough. You have to know the order of the steps and why each step causes the next.

4. Everything builds on everything else

A&P is cumulative. Chemistry explains how cells work. Cells explain how tissues work. Tissues explain how organs such as skin and bone work. Figure 1.1 shows these levels of organization, which you will meet in Chapter 1 of OpenStax. Each step up the ladder depends on the steps below it. A weak spot in Chapter 2 chemistry can show up again weeks later when you study how a nerve cell uses sodium and potassium ions to send a signal.

A&P in action

Here is what the vocabulary load looks like in real life. In OpenStax Chapter 4, one short section on epithelial tissue asks you to know: epithelium, apical surface, basal surface, basement membrane, simple, stratified, pseudostratified, squamous, cuboidal, columnar, cilia, microvilli and keratinized. That is thirteen terms before you reach the next heading. Then you need to match them to places in the body: simple squamous epithelium lines the air sacs of the lungs, where a thin layer lets gases pass quickly, and stratified squamous epithelium covers your skin, where many layers protect you from scrapes. See how the name tells you the structure, and the structure tells you the function? That pattern will save you again and again.

Why smart students struggle at first

Here is something many instructors notice. Students who earned strong grades before are sometimes the ones who are most surprised by their first A&P quiz or exam. That is not because they stopped being smart. It is because the study habits that worked before were built for different kinds of tests.

In many earlier classes, you could do well by reading the notes a few times, highlighting the important parts, and reviewing hard the night before. Those habits work when a test asks you to recognize facts you just saw. A&P exams ask for more. They ask you to recall terms without hints, to apply ideas to a new situation, and to follow a process step by step. A question might describe a patient whose skin is not healing well and ask which layer of the skin was damaged. Rereading your notes does not train you to answer that kind of question. Practicing recall does.

So when a strong student gets a disappointing first grade, the problem is usually the strategy, not the student. Strategy is something you can change this week. That is what the rest of this book is about.

Picture this

Imagine a student named Maya. She was an honor student in high school and works twenty hours a week at a pharmacy. For her first BIO 243 quiz, she read Chapter 1 twice and highlighted almost every page. She felt ready. Her score was a 64. Maya was crushed and wondered whether she should switch majors. Instead, she went to her instructor's student hours and asked what to change. The answer was simple: close the book and test yourself. For the next quiz, Maya made flashcards of the directional terms, practiced them out loud on her break, and drew the body cavities from memory twice. She scored an 86. Same Maya, same brain, different strategy.

A student rereads the chapter three times and still does poorly on a quiz. What is the most likely reason?

  1. The student is not smart enough for A&P.
  2. Rereading builds familiarity, but the quiz asks for recall and application.
  3. The student read too much and confused the material.
  4. Quizzes in A&P are mostly about luck.

Rereading makes material feel familiar, but feeling familiar is not the same as being able to pull the answer out of memory. A&P questions ask you to recall and apply. Chapter 5 explains this "fluency illusion" in more detail.

You belong here, even when it is hard

When a course feels hard, a quiet voice can start asking questions. Am I really a science person? Do the other students get this faster than me? Maybe I am not cut out for nursing. These thoughts are very common, especially for students who are the first in their family to go to college, students who work, and students who have been away from school for a while.

Researchers have studied these worries. Psychologists Gregory Walton and Geoffrey Cohen looked at what happens when students read that worries about belonging are common in the first year of college and usually fade with time. In one study, a short, one-hour activity built around that message was followed by better grades over the next few years for Black students at the university they studied (Walton & Cohen, 2011). Later, a much larger study across many colleges found that a similar online activity helped some students stay enrolled full time, but the benefit was modest and depended on the college and on whether the setting actually offered chances to belong (Walton et al., 2023).

What should you take from this? Not that one activity is magic. The research is more careful than that. The useful message is this: feeling out of place early on is normal, it is shared by many of the students around you, and it does not predict who will succeed. When a hard week makes you doubt yourself, you can read that feeling as a sign that you are learning something difficult, not as proof that you do not belong.

It also helps to know that belonging is something you can build. Learn the names of two classmates in lab. Answer a question in CircleIn. Go to a Supplemental Instruction (SI) session once, just to see what it is like. Introduce yourself to your instructor during student hours. Each small step makes the course feel more like your place.

Myth or fact?

"Some people are just science people, and if I have to work this hard, I am not one of them."

Myth. Everyone who learns A&P well works hard at it, including the classmate who seems to get it instantly. You just do not see their hours with flashcards. Effort in A&P is not a sign of low ability. It is how the learning happens. You may have heard of "growth mindset," the idea that believing your abilities can grow helps you learn. The research on growth mindset programs shows smaller effects than many popular articles claim, so do not expect a belief alone to change your grade. What does change your grade is using strong study strategies, steadily, and asking for help early.

How BIO 243 is built

Knowing how the course is put together helps you plan. Always check your own syllabus and Blackboard course for exact details, points and due dates, because these can change from term to term. In general, here is what you can expect.

The pieces of the course

  • Lecture. Your instructor explains the main ideas and shows you what matters most. Read the assigned OpenStax chapter before class so lecture becomes your second pass through the material, not your first.
  • Lab. Lab is part of your course grade. You work with models, microscopes and activities that turn the textbook into something you can see and touch. Lab needs its own study time, which is why the syllabus asks for about 4 to 6 hours a week for lab in addition to lecture study.
  • Quizzes. Quizzes are taken online in Blackboard. Each one is open for a while before it is due, but quizzes cannot be made up after the due date. A few of your lowest quiz scores are usually dropped, which gives you a small safety net for a bad week. Do not spend that safety net in week 2.
  • Guided Practice. This is the course homework. It walks you through the chapter content in small steps. Treat it as practice, and do it on your own.
  • The OpenStax Reading Guide. This is a fillable guide to the textbook, written by M. Storm, that you will find in Blackboard. Its answers come straight from the textbook, and its content shows up on exams. Completing it before an exam can earn extra credit. Even without the extra credit, it is one of the best study tools you have.
  • CircleIn. CircleIn is a study-community app that USC Upstate pays for, with no ads. In BIO 243 it is graded. You can make flashcards, share notes, and ask and answer questions with classmates.
  • Exams. There are three unit exams and a cumulative final. The exams are multiple choice. The final mostly covers the whole course, plus some new material from the last chapters.

Why starting strong matters so much

The syllabus says it plainly: it is very hard to recover from a low test grade. Each exam is a big share of your grade, and there are only a few of them. If you score well on Exam 1, you have room to breathe for the rest of the term. If you score poorly, every later exam has to carry more weight. That is not meant to scare you. It is meant to show you where your effort pays off most. Steady work in weeks 1 to 4 is the single best investment you can make in this course.

Your first two weeks

The first two weeks set the tone for the whole term. Here is a short list of what successful students tend to do early.

  1. Read the whole syllabus once, slowly. Write the exam weeks, the quiz pattern and the late-work rules on one page you can see every day.
  2. Explore Blackboard. Find where the quizzes, Guided Practice and the Reading Guide live. Turn on notifications.
  3. Set up CircleIn. Log in, find the BIO 243 class, and look at how flashcards and questions work.
  4. Open the OpenStax textbook. It is free online. Bookmark it on your phone so you can study in small moments.
  5. Start Chapter 1 right away. The early vocabulary (directional terms, body planes, body cavities and homeostasis) shows up in every chapter after it.
  6. Block your study time. Chapter 2 of this book helps you find 8 to 12 real hours in a busy week.

A&P in action

Homeostasis is one of the first big ideas in BIO 243, and it comes back in almost every chapter. Homeostasis means keeping the body's internal conditions within a healthy range. Most of it works by negative feedback. When your body temperature rises on a hot day, sensors in the skin and brain detect the change. A control center in the brain, the hypothalamus, sends signals to effectors: sweat glands release sweat, and blood vessels in the skin widen so heat can escape. Your temperature drops back toward its set point, and the response slows down. Sensor, control center, effector, response. Learn that pattern now. You will see it again with skin, bone, muscle and nerves.

Try it now

Close this page or look away from your notes. On a scrap of paper, write the opposite of each directional term: superior, anterior, medial, proximal, superficial. Then check OpenStax Chapter 1. If you got all five, nice work. If you missed some, you just found out what to practice, and that took only two minutes. This small act of testing yourself is the most important habit in this whole book.

What success looks like in BIO 243

Success in A&P does not mean never feeling lost. It means having a plan for when you do. Students who do well in this course tend to share a few habits, and none of them depend on talent:

  • They study a little almost every day instead of a lot on one day.
  • They test themselves instead of only rereading.
  • They keep up with quizzes, Guided Practice and the Reading Guide so nothing piles up.
  • They ask questions early, in class, in student hours, in SI and in CircleIn.
  • They look at each quiz and exam result as information about what to change, not as a verdict about who they are.

Many of you are balancing a job, a long commute or care for family. Those responsibilities are real, and this book will not pretend you have unlimited free time. Instead, it will help you get the most learning out of the time you do have. Short, focused, well-planned study sessions beat long, tired ones. You do not need a perfect week. You need a workable one, repeated.

And remember why you are here. The terms you learn in BIO 243 are the words nurses, therapists, technicians and physicians use every day. The homeostasis loop you learn this week is the same loop a nurse watches when a patient runs a fever. The tissue types you learn in Chapter 4 explain why some injuries heal fast and others heal slowly. This course is hard because it matters. You can do it, one well-planned week at a time.

Chapter 2

Your Real Week

Time, energy and a plan that survives real life

Everyone gets the same 168 hours in a week, but no two students spend them the same way. This chapter helps you find real study time inside your real life, with its job, commute, family and need for sleep, and build a weekly plan that bends instead of breaking.

The 168-hour week

Seven days times twenty-four hours is 168 hours. That number sounds big until you start to fill it in. Sleep takes about a third of it. Classes and lab take more. Many BIO 243 students also work, drive to campus, care for children or parents, or help run a household. By the time those hours are counted, the time left for studying can feel very small.

That is exactly why it helps to count. Most people guess wrong about where their time goes. Some think they have no free time at all, and then discover two hours a day lost to their phone. Others think they have plenty of time, and then discover that work and commuting eat most of their afternoons. A plan built on guesses breaks in the first week. A plan built on real numbers has a chance.

Figure 2.1 shows one possible week for a fictional full-time student who works part time. Your numbers will be different, and that is the point. The figure is a model for the exercise you are about to do.

Try it now

Grab a piece of paper and write down your fixed hours for a normal week. Start with sleep (aim for at least 7 hours a night, so at least 49). Add class and lab hours, work hours, commuting time, and time you must spend caring for others. Add about 3 hours a day for meals, showers and getting ready. Subtract the total from 168. The number you are left with is the pool your study time and free time must come from. Write it down. You will use it in a few minutes.

How much study BIO 243 really needs

Your BIO 243 syllabus asks for about 4 to 6 hours a week of study for lecture, plus 4 to 6 hours a week for lab. That is 8 to 12 hours a week outside of class for this one course. For many students, that is more than they have ever studied for a single class. It is also realistic. A&P has a heavy vocabulary load, a fast pace and a cumulative final, and the hours are what it takes to keep up.

What counts as study time

Study time is any time you are actively working with BIO 243 material. That includes:

  • Reading the OpenStax chapter before class, and working on the Reading Guide.
  • Doing Guided Practice and taking quizzes.
  • Rewriting and reconciling your notes after lecture (Chapter 4 shows you how).
  • Making and practicing flashcards, including in CircleIn.
  • Reviewing lab models, slides and terms between lab sessions.
  • Going to Supplemental Instruction (SI) sessions or tutoring.

Time sitting in lecture or lab does not count toward the 8 to 12. Neither does time with the textbook open while you text, watch a show or scroll. Study hours are about attention, not about how long a book sits on the table.

An honest talk about your load

If you did the math in the box above and found only 4 or 5 free hours in your whole week, you have learned something important. It does not mean you cannot take A&P. It does mean something has to give, and it is far better to face that now than after Exam 1. You might be able to shift work hours, find study time on your commute, or get help with a responsibility at home. Sometimes the right answer is a lighter course load this term so you can give A&P the time it needs. That is a decision to talk through with your academic advisor and, if you receive aid, with Financial Aid, because changing your load can affect both. Chapter 13 covers how to have those conversations.

Building your weekly template

A weekly template is a picture of a normal week, with study blocks placed on purpose. You build it once, then reuse it every week, adjusting for exam weeks and surprises. Students who study at the same times each week spend less energy deciding when to study, and more energy actually studying.

Step by step

  1. Put in sleep first. Before anything else, block the hours you will sleep. Sleep is not wasted time. It is when your brain stores what you learned that day (more on that below and in Chapter 5).
  2. Add the fixed blocks. Class, lab, work shifts, commuting, and family duties that cannot move.
  3. Place a short study block soon after each BIO 243 lecture. Even 30 to 45 minutes on the same day to rewrite and reconcile your notes makes the material stick far better than waiting a week.
  4. Place reading blocks before lecture. Reading the chapter before class turns lecture into your second look at the material.
  5. Add one longer block for practice. Once a week, plan 90 minutes to 2 hours for deeper work, such as drawing processes or doing practice questions.
  6. Add a weekly review block. Pick a time at the end or start of each week (many people choose Sunday) to review the week's chapters and plan the next week.
  7. Leave some white space. A plan with no free time breaks the first time anything goes wrong. Leave a buffer.

Energy matters as much as time

An hour is not always an hour. One hour when you are rested and alert can be worth two hours when you are exhausted after a closing shift. So as you place study blocks, think about energy, not just time on the clock.

Many people feel most alert in the late morning, feel a dip in the early afternoon (especially after a big lunch), and get a second wind in the early evening before energy fades at night. Figure 2.3 sketches that common pattern. But people differ. Some are true morning people, and some think best late at night. Your job is to notice your own pattern and protect your best hours for your hardest work.

Match the task to the energy

Not all study tasks need the same amount of brain power. Reading a new chapter for the first time, working through a hard process, or drawing a diagram from memory takes a lot. Running through flashcards you mostly know, organizing your notes, or checking due dates in Blackboard takes much less. When you know which is which, you can match them to the right time of day.

A&P in action

Think about OpenStax Chapter 3, Cells. Following protein synthesis from DNA to mRNA (transcription) and from mRNA to a chain of amino acids (translation) is heavy, new thinking. Give it a peak-energy block, with paper so you can draw the steps as a flow chart. Reviewing the names and jobs of the organelles (the mitochondria make most of the cell's ATP, ribosomes build proteins, the Golgi apparatus modifies and packages them) is lighter work once you have learned them the first time. That fits a low-energy slot, like ten minutes of flashcards after a work shift.

Use small moments, safely

If you ride a bus, wait in a pickup line or sit through a break at work, you have small pockets of time. Five to ten minutes of flashcards on your phone in those pockets adds up to hours over a semester. Keep a CircleIn deck or a photo of your vocabulary list ready for those moments. One firm rule: if you drive, the commute is not study time for reading or flashcards. Keep your eyes on the road.

Small blocks beat marathons

Long study marathons feel serious, but attention does not last for hours at a time. Most people focus best in blocks of about 25 to 50 minutes followed by a short break. A popular version is the Pomodoro Technique, created by Francesco Cirillo: work for 25 minutes on one task, take a 5-minute break, and repeat, with a longer break after four rounds. You do not have to follow it exactly. The idea is what matters: a clear start, a clear stop, and one task at a time.

How to make a block work

  • Name one task. "Study A&P" is too vague. "Make 15 flashcards from the Chapter 4 connective tissue table" is a task you can start and finish.
  • Put the phone out of reach. Silence it and put it in another room or a bag. Even a phone sitting face down on the table can pull at your attention.
  • Use a timer. Knowing a break is coming makes it easier to stay focused now.
  • Take a real break. Stand up, stretch, refill your water. Scrolling social media is not much of a break for your brain and easily turns 5 minutes into 30.
  • End by testing yourself. Spend the last few minutes of each block closing your notes and recalling what you just studied.

Write "if, then" plans

One of the most useful findings about planning comes from research on implementation intentions. These are plans written in an "if, then" form: "If it is Tuesday at noon, then I go to the Library and practice my Chapter 4 flashcards." Many studies have found that people who make specific "if, then" plans are more likely to follow through on their goals than people who only state the goal (Gollwitzer, 1999; Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006). The plan works because it links the action to a clear cue, so you do not have to decide all over again each time.

Which study plan is written in the most useful way?

  1. "I will study A&P more this week."
  2. "I will study A&P for five hours on Sunday."
  3. "I will try to look at my notes when I have time."
  4. "If it is Monday after lunch, then I read OpenStax 4.2 at the Library and fill in that part of the Reading Guide."

The last plan names a cue (Monday after lunch), a place and one specific task. That kind of "if, then" plan is the easiest to follow through on. One giant Sunday session is a cram session, and vague plans rarely happen.

The "never cram" logic

The syllabus says not to cram, and that is not just a nice idea. Here is the reasoning. Imagine two students who each study 8 hours for Exam 1. One studies 8 hours the two days before the exam. The other studies about an hour a day across the two weeks before the exam. The total time is the same. But decades of research show that spreading study out over time, called spacing or distributed practice, leads to much better long-term memory than packing the same time into one session (Cepeda et al., 2006).

Cramming can sometimes get you through a test the next morning. But much of that learning fades within days, and BIO 243 has a cumulative final. Material you crammed for Exam 1 will be mostly gone by the final, and you will have to learn it all over again. Material you spaced out will still be there, and reviewing it will be fast. Spacing is not just a better way to study. Over a whole semester, it is less total work.

Chapter 5 explains why memory works this way, and Chapter 6 shows you exactly how to space your practice. For now, the planning lesson is simple: study BIO 243 a little on most days, not a lot on one day.

Myth or fact?

"I work better under pressure, so I study best the night before."

Mostly myth. Pressure can make you feel focused, and a deadline does get you started. But feeling focused is not the same as learning well. Last-minute study tends to cut into sleep, leaves no time to find and fix gaps, and does not last until the final. If deadlines help you get started, make your own: set small, early deadlines in your weekly template, like "Reading Guide section done by Wednesday night."

Protect your sleep

When time is tight, sleep is often the first thing students cut. That is a costly trade. An expert panel convened by the National Sleep Foundation recommends 7 to 9 hours of sleep a night for young adults and adults (Hirshkowitz et al., 2015). Sleep is not just rest. While you sleep, your brain works to stabilize and organize memories from the day, which is part of how studying turns into long-term learning.

The research with college students points in the same direction. One study tracked students in a college chemistry course with wearable sleep trackers and found that students who slept more, and more consistently, across the term tended to earn better grades (Okano et al., 2019). That kind of study shows a link, not proof that sleep alone caused the higher grades, but it fits with a large body of lab research on sleep and memory.

Here are a few ways to protect your sleep in a busy week:

  • Block your sleep in the template first, and treat it like a work shift you cannot skip.
  • Try to keep a similar bedtime and wake time most days, including weekends.
  • Stop heavy studying 30 to 60 minutes before bed. A short, calm flashcard review is fine.
  • Avoid caffeine late in the day. Chapter 12 looks at caffeine and energy drinks honestly.

When the plan breaks

Your plan will break. A child gets sick, a boss asks you to cover a shift, the car will not start, a quiz takes longer than you expected. This is not a sign you are bad at planning. It is a sign you have a real life. The question is not whether the plan will break, but what you do when it does.

Expect things to take longer

Psychologists have a name for our habit of underestimating how long tasks will take: the planning fallacy. In classic studies, students predicting how long it would take to finish a major project were too optimistic, even when they knew similar projects had run late in the past (Buehler et al., 1994). The fix is simple. When you plan a task, add a buffer, and leave some open time in your week that is not assigned to anything.

The most important box in that chart is "shrink it." When you cannot do the full block, do a 15-minute version instead: one section of the Reading Guide, or one pass through a flashcard deck. They fall behind because one missed block turns into "I will catch up this weekend," and then the weekend fills up too.

The weekly review

Once a week, sit down for 15 to 20 minutes with your template, your syllabus and Blackboard. Ask three questions. What is due this coming week? Which study blocks worked last week, and which did not? Chapter 3

Reading OpenStax Like a Scientist

Preview, chunk, explain, and read the figures

Reading a science textbook is not like reading a novel or a news story, and nobody is born knowing how to do it. This chapter gives you a step-by-step way to read OpenStax Anatomy and Physiology so that the words actually stay with you, and so that the figures, which carry half of the content, start to make sense.

Textbook reading is a skill

Many students say the same thing about their first A&P reading: "I read the whole section, and then I could not tell you a single thing it said." If that has happened to you, you are not a bad reader. You were reading a science textbook the way most of us read everything else, from start to finish at a steady pace, waiting for the meaning to arrive. That works for a story. It does not work for a page that introduces ten new terms, describes a process with six steps, and points to a figure with fourteen labels.

Scientists and health professionals read differently. They read with a purpose. They look over the whole thing first. They read in small pieces and stop to check that each piece makes sense. They study the figures as carefully as the words. And they put what they read into their own words before moving on. None of that is talent. It is a set of habits, and you can learn them this week.

One more thing before we start: slow is normal. A dense A&P section can take two or three times longer to read well than a page of a novel. If you plan 20 minutes for a section that really needs 45, you will feel behind and rush. When you build your weekly template (Chapter 2), give reading more time than you think it needs.

Before you read: a five-minute preview

Before you read a section closely, spend about five minutes looking it over. A preview gives your brain a map, so that each new fact has a place to land. It is the first step in a well-known reading method called SQ3R (survey, question, read, recite, review), which was described by Francis Robinson in the 1940s and is still taught today.

When you preview an OpenStax chapter, look at these parts in order:

  1. The learning objectives. Each OpenStax section begins with a short list that starts "By the end of this section, you will be able to..." These are a gift. They tell you exactly what the authors think matters most, and they line up closely with what you will be asked to do.
  2. The headings. Read the section title and every subheading. Together they form an outline of the section.
  3. The bold terms. Skim for words in bold. These are the key terms that also appear in the chapter's key terms list. Do not try to learn them yet. Just notice how many there are.
  4. The figures and captions. Look at each figure and read its caption. You will come back to them, but a first look tells you what the section is about faster than any paragraph.
  5. The end-of-chapter questions. Glance at the review questions and critical thinking questions. They show you the kind of thinking you will need.

Turn headings into questions

The second step in SQ3R is to turn what you previewed into questions. This simple move changes how you read. Instead of passively taking in words, you are hunting for answers. Here are some examples from BIO 243 chapters:

  • "Epithelial Tissue" becomes "What are the kinds of epithelial tissue, and how does each one's shape fit its job?"
  • "The Cell Membrane" becomes "What is the membrane made of, and how do things get across it?"
  • "Bone Formation and Development" becomes "What are the two ways bones form, and what cells do the work?"
  • "Muscle Fiber Contraction and Relaxation" becomes "What steps happen, in order, when a muscle fiber contracts?"

Write your questions in the margin of your notes or at the top of a page. When you finish reading the section, you should be able to answer them without looking. If you cannot, you know exactly what to reread.

Try it now

Open OpenStax Chapter 4, Section 4.3, "Connective Tissue Supports and Protects." Spend two minutes on a preview only: read the learning objectives, the subheadings, and the captions of the first two figures. Then write two questions you expect the section to answer. For example: "What are the three parts of connective tissue?" and "How are loose and dense connective tissue different?" Keep the questions. You will use them when you read.

Read in chunks, then stop and explain

After the preview, read the section one chunk at a time. A chunk is usually one subsection, or two or three paragraphs: just enough for one main idea. At the end of each chunk, stop. Look away from the page. Then do one of two things.

Recite: say it without looking

Try to say or write the main idea of the chunk from memory, in your own words. Researchers tested a simple routine called read, recite, review. Students read a passage, then recited out loud everything they could remember, then reread the passage to check. Compared with simply rereading or taking notes, the read-recite-review routine led to better recall and better performance on test questions, and it took no extra materials at all (McDaniel et al., 2009). Reciting works because it is a small act of retrieval practice, the most powerful study strategy you will meet in Chapter 6.

Self-explain: ask "why" and "how"

The second move is to explain the chunk to yourself. Ask: Why is this true? How does this connect to what I already know? What would happen if this step failed? In a classic study, students who were prompted to explain each line of a biology text to themselves as they read understood the material better than students who read it twice (Chi et al., 1994). A later review of many studies found that prompting self-explanation helps learning across many subjects, with a moderate overall benefit (Bisra et al., 2018). In A&P, self-explanation is how you move past memorizing names to understanding how the body works.

Myth or fact?

"Highlighting is a good way to study a textbook."

Mostly myth. A major review of study techniques rated highlighting and underlining as having low usefulness for learning (Dunlosky et al., 2013). The problem is that highlighting feels like work while letting you skip the hard part: deciding what matters and putting it in your own words. Many students also highlight far too much, so the page ends up mostly yellow. If you like to highlight, use it only after you have read and recited a chunk, and mark just the one or two terms you could not recall. Then turn those into flashcards.

You just finished reading a subsection on the cell membrane. According to this chapter, what is the best thing to do next?

  1. Highlight the most important sentences and move on.
  2. Look away and say the main idea in your own words, then ask yourself why it works that way.
  3. Read the subsection again from the beginning, slowly.
  4. Copy the bold terms into your notes exactly as written.

Reciting from memory and then self-explaining are the two moves with research support. Rereading and copying feel productive, but they do not test whether you can recall or explain anything.

Practice: paraphrasing a Chapter 4 passage

Let's practice on a short passage written in the style of OpenStax Chapter 4. Read it once (it is set in italics below), then look away and try to say it in your own words before reading on.

Connective tissue is made of three main parts: cells, protein fibers and ground substance. The fibers and the ground substance together form the extracellular matrix, which fills the space between the cells. Fibroblasts are the most common cells in connective tissue proper; they make the fibers and the ground substance. Collagen fibers are strong and resist pulling forces. Elastic fibers can stretch and then return to their original shape. Reticular fibers form a fine supporting mesh inside soft organs. The type and amount of fibers and ground substance determine the properties of each connective tissue.

A weak paraphrase

"Connective tissue has cells, fibers and ground substance. There are collagen, elastic and reticular fibers."

This is not wrong, but it is mostly a list of names. It does not say what the matrix is, who makes it, or why the fibers matter. A student with this paraphrase could answer "name the three fiber types" but would struggle with a question like "Which fiber would you expect to find in large amounts in a tendon, and why?"

A strong paraphrase

"Connective tissue is mostly not cells. It is cells sitting in a matrix that they make themselves, mainly fibroblasts. The matrix has a gel-like ground substance plus fibers. Each fiber does a different job: collagen is for strength against pulling (so tendons should be full of it), elastic is for stretch and snap back (like in skin or artery walls), and reticular makes a soft net that holds organs together. So if I know what job a tissue has to do, I can predict which fibers it has."

Notice what the strong paraphrase does. It is in plain words. It connects structure to function. It makes a prediction (tendons need collagen). And it ends with a rule that will help on questions the student has never seen. That last sentence is self-explanation at work.

A&P in action

The strong paraphrase above sets you up for a classic exam question. Tendons connect muscle to bone, and they have to resist huge pulling forces every time the muscle contracts. That is why tendons are made of dense regular connective tissue, packed with collagen fibers that all run in the same direction, parallel to the pull. Compare that to the dermis of your skin, which is pulled in many directions and has dense irregular connective tissue, with collagen fibers running every which way. Same fiber, different arrangement, because the job is different. When you read with "Why is it built this way?" in mind, questions like this stop being memorization and start being logic.

Build a running vocabulary list with word roots

A&P vocabulary looks scary until you notice that most of it is built from a fairly small set of Greek and Latin parts. A prefix comes at the front of a word, a root carries the core meaning, and a suffix comes at the end. Learn a part once, and it unlocks dozens of words for the rest of the course and the rest of your career.

How to keep the list

Keep one running vocabulary list for the whole course, in a notebook, a document or a CircleIn deck. For each new term, write four things:

  1. The term, spelled correctly.
  2. Its word parts and what they mean, if it has any.
  3. A short definition in your own words.
  4. Where it is or what it does in the body.

Here are some word parts you will meet again and again in BIO 243:

  • osteo- (bone): osteocyte, osteoclast, osteoporosis
  • chondr- (cartilage): chondrocyte, perichondrium
  • myo- or sarco- (muscle): myofibril, sarcomere, sarcoplasmic reticulum
  • neuro- (nerve): neuron, neurotransmitter, neuromuscular junction
  • -cyte (cell), -blast (builder or immature cell), -clast (breaker)
  • hyper- (above, more), hypo- (below, less): hypertrophy, hypodermis
  • -itis (inflammation): dermatitis, arthritis, osteomyelitis
  • peri- (around), endo- (within), epi- (upon): periosteum, endosteum, epimysium

Look at that last line. The periosteum is the membrane around a bone, and the endosteum lines the space inside it. Later, in muscle, the epimysium wraps a whole muscle, the perimysium wraps bundles of fibers, and the endomysium surrounds each single fiber. Once you know the prefixes, the names tell you exactly where each layer is.

Try it now

Without looking anything up, use the word parts above to guess the meaning of these three terms: chondroblast, osteoarthritis and perineurium. Then check them in OpenStax. (Chondroblast: a cell that builds cartilage. Osteoarthritis: a joint condition involving bone; the word part says inflammation, but in OpenStax you will see it is mainly wear and breakdown of joint cartilage. Perineurium: the wrapping around a bundle of nerve fibers.) If you got even one, you just decoded a word you had never studied.

Using word parts, what would you predict an osteoclast does?

  1. Breaks down bone tissue
  2. Builds new cartilage
  3. Wraps around a muscle fiber
  4. Carries nerve signals in bone

Osteo- means bone and -clast means breaker. Osteoclasts break down (resorb) bone, while osteoblasts build it. That balance is how bones remodel throughout life, which you will study in OpenStax Chapter 6.

Reading figures: half the content lives here

In many subjects, pictures are decoration. In A&P, they are the content. A single OpenStax figure of a sarcomere, a neuron or a layer of skin can hold as much information as a full page of text, and exam questions often describe or show the same kinds of structures. If you skip the figures, you skip half the chapter.

Research on learning from words and pictures, often called multimedia learning, finds that people generally learn more deeply from words combined with well-designed pictures than from words alone (Mayer, 2009). But that only works if you actually study the picture. Here is how.

Four steps for any figure

  1. Read the caption first. The caption tells you what the figure shows and often adds facts that are not in the main text.
  2. Find every label. Trace each label line to the exact structure it points to. Say the names out loud. Notice which labels are also bold terms in the text.
  3. Check the scale. Is it a drawing or a photo? Many A&P figures include micrographs, which are photos taken through a microscope. "LM" means light micrograph, the kind you will see in lab. "SEM" and "TEM" mean electron micrographs, which show much smaller details. The magnification tells you how big things really are.
  4. Link the shape to the job. Ask why the structure looks the way it does. Simple squamous epithelium is thin and flat because its job is to let gases and fluids pass through quickly. Stratified squamous epithelium is thick because its job is protection.

Turn figures into a quiz

Once you have studied a figure, cover the labels with sticky notes or a strip of paper and try to name each structure. You can also take a screenshot, use a photo editor to cover the labels, and quiz yourself from your phone. This "blank diagram" practice is one of the best ways to prepare for both exams and lab practicals, and Chapter 7 shows you how to do it well.

Chapter 4

Notes That Work

Reconcile, rewrite, map and draw your way to real understanding

Good notes are not a copy of the lecture or the textbook. They are a tool you build for one purpose: to help your future self understand, remember and test yourself on BIO 243. This chapter shows you how to take notes in lecture and while reading, how to combine them the way the syllabus asks, and how to turn them into maps, flow charts, drawings and self-tests.

What notes are really for

Ask a room of students why they take notes and most will say, "So I have something to study later." That is true, but it is only half the story. Notes do two jobs. The first job is to record: to capture information you might otherwise lose, like an example your instructor gave or a figure the lecture focused on. The second job is to transform: to make you process the material, by choosing what matters, putting it in your own words and connecting it to what you already know.

The second job is where most of the learning happens. A page of notes copied word for word from a slide records a lot but transforms almost nothing. A shorter page where you wrote "Osteoblasts build bone, osteoclasts break it down: blast builds, clast crashes" did both jobs at once. As you read this chapter, keep asking: Is this method making me think, or just making me write?

Lecture notes and reading notes are different

In BIO 243 you will build two kinds of notes, and they have different strengths.

Reading notes

Reading notes come from the OpenStax textbook, ideally before the lecture on that topic. You make them at your own pace, so they can be careful and complete. They are the place for definitions, the details of a process, and your vocabulary list with word roots (Chapter 3). Their weakness is that they do not tell you what your instructor thinks is most important, and they can easily become a long, flat copy of the book.

Lecture notes

Lecture notes capture what happens in class: what your instructor emphasizes, the examples and clinical stories they use, the connections they draw between topics, and the questions other students ask. They are fast and messy by nature, because you cannot pause a lecture. Their weakness is that they have gaps. You will miss words, mishear terms and fall behind during a fast stretch.

Tips for taking lecture notes

  • Do not try to write everything. You cannot keep up, and trying makes you stop thinking. Capture key terms, main ideas, examples and anything your instructor repeats, draws or slows down for.
  • Leave space. Skip lines and leave a wide margin. You will fill those gaps later.
  • Mark confusion right away. Put a "?" next to anything that did not make sense. That mark becomes a question for your reconcile session, for SI or for student hours.
  • Use the slides if they are posted. If your instructor shares slides before class, download them and write your notes on or beside them, so you can listen instead of copying.
  • Write down the examples. When your instructor says something like "Think about what happens when you get a paper cut," write the example down. Clinical examples are often the bridge between a term and an exam question.

A&P in action

Suppose the OpenStax reading on bone (Chapter 6) describes compact bone, the osteon, the central canal, lamellae, lacunae and canaliculi. That is a lot of terms, and your reading notes will list them. Then in lecture, your instructor sketches an osteon on the board as a target with rings, draws tiny "spiders" in the rings for the osteocytes sitting in lacunae, and says, "The canaliculi are the spider legs. They let these cells pass nutrients and wastes, because bone is too hard for anything to soak through." That one sentence explains why the canaliculi exist. It is not in your reading notes. It is the kind of thing your lecture notes are for, and it is exactly what you will want when you reconcile the two.

The reconcile-and-rewrite routine

Your BIO 243 syllabus asks you to rewrite and reconcile your notes from reading and lecture. This is one of the most valuable habits you can build in the course. "Reconcile" means to bring two things into agreement. You lay your reading notes and lecture notes side by side, find where they match, where they differ and where each has gaps, and then write one clean, combined version in your own words.

Why does this work so well? Because it forces exactly the kind of thinking that builds memory. You have to decide what matters. You have to explain things in your own words. You have to notice and fix what you do not understand. And you see the material a second and third time, spaced out from the first, which helps it last.

How to do it, step by step

  1. Do it soon. Aim for the same day as lecture, or within a day. The lecture is still fresh, so you can fill in gaps from memory.
  2. Lay both sets side by side. Open your reading notes and your lecture notes, plus the OpenStax section.
  3. Mark three things. Put a check where they agree. Circle anything in one set that is missing from the other. Find every "?" you wrote in lecture.
  4. Fix the "?" marks. Use OpenStax to answer them. If you cannot, write the question on a separate list to bring to SI, CircleIn or student hours.
  5. Rewrite one clean page. Close your notes for a moment and try to write the main ideas from memory first. Then open them and fill in what you missed. Use your own words, short sentences, and drawings or flow charts where they help.
  6. Add questions. In the margin, write questions that the page answers. These become your self-test later.

A full reconcile session for one lecture usually takes 30 to 45 minutes. That is why Chapter 2 suggested placing a study block soon after each BIO 243 lecture. This is what that block is for.

Cornell-style notes: build the questions in

One of the best formats for your rewritten page is the Cornell system, developed by Walter Pauk at Cornell University and still taught in study-skills courses (Pauk & Owens, 2014). The page is divided into three parts:

  • A wide notes column on the right for your main notes.
  • A narrow cue column on the left, where you write questions or key words that match each part of the notes.
  • A summary strip across the bottom, where you write two or three sentences that pull the whole page together.

The real power of Cornell notes is the cue column. When you study later, fold the page or cover the notes column with a sheet of paper. Read each cue question and answer it out loud from memory. Then uncover the notes and check. Your notes have just become a self-test, with no extra work. The summary strip matters too. Writing two sentences that capture the whole page forces you to find the big idea, which is exactly what you need to answer application questions.

Try it now

Take any page of BIO 243 notes you already have. Draw a vertical line about a third of the way in from the left edge (or fold the page). For each chunk of notes on the right, write one question in the left margin that the chunk answers. Aim for at least three questions, and make at least one start with "Why" or "What happens if." Then cover the right side and try to answer them. You have just turned an old page into a practice quiz.

Concept maps: show how ideas connect

A&P is full of categories inside categories. There are four types of tissue, each with several subtypes, each with its own structure, location and function. A list is a poor way to see all of that at once. A concept map is better. It puts each idea in a box or bubble and connects the boxes with lines that are labeled with a short phrase, such as "includes," "lines," or "is made of." Those link labels are what make it a concept map instead of just a doodle. They force you to state exactly how two ideas are related.

The key: build it from memory

Concept maps have good research support. A review of many studies found that students who studied with concept maps tended to remember more than students who studied with lists, outlines or text alone (Nesbit & Adesope, 2006). But there is an important twist. In one well-known experiment, students who built a concept map while looking at a science text did worse on a later test than students who practiced recalling the text from memory (Karpicke & Blunt, 2011). The lesson is not that maps are bad. It is that a map you copy is weak, and a map you build from memory is strong. Close the book, draw what you can, then open the book and add what you missed in a different color. The gaps you fill in are exactly what you need to study.

Which way of using a concept map is most likely to help you on a BIO 243 exam?

  1. Copy the map from a classmate's CircleIn post.
  2. Draw a map while reading straight from the textbook, then put it away.
  3. Look at a map in the textbook several times.
  4. Draw the map from memory, then check the book and fill gaps in a different color.

Building the map from memory turns it into retrieval practice, and checking afterward shows you exactly what to study. Copying or just looking at a map does not make you pull anything out of memory.

Flow charts: turn processes into steps

Your syllabus asks you to draw processes as flow charts, and there is a good reason. Physiology is full of processes: chains of events where each step causes the next. Paragraphs hide that structure. A flow chart shows it. Each box is one event, and each arrow means "which causes" or "which leads to."

Here is how to build one. First, list the steps in order on scrap paper. Then put each step in a box, using as few words as you can. Connect the boxes with arrows. Finally, test the chart by reading it aloud with the word "because" between each pair of boxes. If a "because" does not make sense, a step is missing or out of order.

A&P in action

Figure 4.4 shows why flow charts beat paragraphs for physiology. In the textbook, calcium regulation is spread across several paragraphs about parathyroid hormone (PTH), bone, the kidneys and vitamin D. On a flow chart, you can see that one trigger (low blood calcium) leads to one hormone with three different targets, all pushing calcium the same way. You can also see the loop close: once calcium is back to normal, PTH release drops. An exam question might ask what would happen to bone if PTH levels stayed high for a long time. With the chart in your head, you can reason it out: osteoclasts keep breaking down bone, so bone would lose mass.

Drawing and labeling

You do not have to be an artist to learn A&P by drawing. Research on drawing and memory has found that drawing something tends to help people remember it better than writing it down, likely because drawing makes you think about what it looks like, what it means and how to show it, all at once (Fernandes et al., 2018). In BIO 243, simple sketches are enough: a neuron as a blob with branches and one long tail, a sarcomere as a row of lines between two Z discs, the layers of the skin as stacked bands.

The best drawing practice works the same way as the best concept maps. First, look at the OpenStax figure and study it. Then put it away and draw it from memory, with labels. Then compare and fix what you missed. Repeat a day or two later. Chapter 7 goes deeper on how to learn structures and processes by drawing from memory, and how to use it for lab practicals.

Laptop, tablet or paper?

You may have heard that handwritten notes are always better than typed ones. That idea comes largely from a famous study in which students who took notes on laptops tended to copy lectures word for word and did worse on some conceptual questions than students who wrote by hand (Mueller & Oppenheimer, 2014). The study got a lot of attention. But later research has been more mixed. A replication that also added new conditions found the handwriting advantage was smaller and less consistent than first reported (Morehead et al., 2019), and a direct replication with a combined look at similar studies did not find a clear overall advantage for longhand (Urry et al., 2021).

So what should you do? Here is the honest summary:

  • The tool matters less than what you do with it. Copying word for word is weak on any device. Choosing, summarizing and connecting ideas is strong on any device.
  • Drawing is easier by hand or with a stylus. A&P has a lot of diagrams and flow charts, which is a real point in favor of paper or a tablet with a pen.
  • Distraction is the bigger danger. In one study, students who multitasked on laptops during a lecture learned less, and so did students sitting nearby who could see their screens (Sana et al., 2013). If you use a laptop, close every tab and app that is not your notes.
  • Use what you will actually reconcile. The best notes are the ones you return to the same day.

Myth or fact?

"Science proved that typing your notes is bad for learning."

Overstated. One influential study found an advantage for handwriting, but later, larger studies found the difference was small or unclear. What does seem clear is that copying word for word is a weak strategy, and that having social media or games open during class hurts learning for you and the people around you. Choose the tool that helps you think and draw, and protect your attention either way.

Turning notes into self-test material

Your notes reach their full value when you use them to test yourself, not just to reread. Once you have a reconciled page, you can turn it into practice in several ways:

  • Cue questions. Cover the notes column and answer the cue column out loud.
  • Flashcards. Turn each key term into a card with the term on one side and your own definition and an example on the other. CircleIn is a good place to build them, and Chapter 6 explains how to use flashcards the right way.
  • Blank diagrams. Redraw a figure without labels and fill them in from memory.
  • Flow chart rebuilds. Write the first and last box of a process and fill in the middle from memory.
  • Your own exam questions. The syllabus suggests writing your own questions. Write two multiple-choice questions per lecture, with believable wrong answers. Writing good wrong answers forces you to understand the common mix-ups.

When you share notes or flashcards in CircleIn, remember that CircleIn is graded work. What you post should be your own.

Chapter 5

How Memory Actually Works

Encoding, forgetting, and why rereading fools you

If you understand how memory works, the best study strategies stop looking like random advice and start looking like common sense. This chapter explains, in plain words, how information gets into your memory, why most of it slips away, and why the study habits that feel the most productive are often the ones that help the least.

Memory has three jobs

Psychologists often describe memory as doing three jobs. Think of a library.

  • Encoding is getting a book onto the shelf. It is the process of taking in new information and turning it into a form your brain can keep. When you read about the layers of the skin and make sense of them, you are encoding.
  • Storage is keeping the book on the shelf over time. Some memories fade within minutes. Others last for decades. What happens after you study, including sleep, affects how well memories are stored.
  • Retrieval is finding the book again when you need it. On an exam, you have to locate the right information and pull it out, often with only a short question as a clue.

Here is the key insight for BIO 243: most students only practice the first job. They spend their study time reading, rereading and highlighting, which is all encoding. Then the exam asks them to do the third job, retrieval, which they never practiced. It is like training for a basketball game by watching videos of free throws. The watching helps a little, but the game is about shooting.

Working memory is small

Everything you learn passes through working memory, the mental workspace where you hold and handle information right now. When you read a sentence, keep a term in mind, or follow the steps of a process, you are using working memory. It is powerful, but it is small. You may have heard that people can hold "seven, plus or minus two" items at once. Later research suggests the real limit for most people is closer to about four meaningful chunks at a time (Cowan, 2001).

That limit explains a lot about A&P. When a paragraph introduces eight new terms at once, your working memory overflows, and some of them simply never get encoded. It is not that you were not paying attention. Your workspace was full.

Chunking: the way around the limit

The good news is that a "chunk" can be big if it is meaningful. To a beginner, the five layers of the epidermis are five separate items. To someone who understands the skin, they are one chunk: "cells are born at the bottom and get pushed up, flattening, filling with keratin and dying on the way to the surface." That one idea holds all five layers. This is why understanding beats memorizing. Understanding packs many facts into a few chunks, which frees up working memory for the next new idea.

Working memory limits also mean that distractions are costly. A text message, a TV in the background or a second tab uses some of your limited workspace, leaving less room for the A&P you are trying to learn.

A&P in action

The layers of the epidermis in thick skin, from deep to surface, are the stratum basale, stratum spinosum, stratum granulosum, stratum lucidum and stratum corneum (OpenStax Chapter 5). Trying to memorize those as five Latin names is hard on working memory. Turn them into a story instead. In the stratum basale (base), stem cells divide. New cells get pushed up into the stratum spinosum, where they look spiny under the microscope. In the stratum granulosum, they fill with granules and begin to die. The stratum lucidum (clear) is a thin clear layer found only in thick skin, like the palms and soles. At the top, the stratum corneum is many layers of flat, dead, keratin-filled cells that protect you and are constantly shed. One story, one chunk, five layers.

Why does reading a paragraph with ten new A&P terms often leave you remembering only a few?

  1. Long-term memory fills up quickly and has to empty out.
  2. Most people can only learn terms by hearing them, not by reading.
  3. Working memory can only handle a few chunks at once, so it overflows.
  4. Terms are only remembered if they are highlighted.

Working memory holds only about four meaningful chunks at a time. Ten brand-new terms overload it. Reading in small chunks and connecting terms into meaningful stories helps you get around the limit. Long-term memory is not the bottleneck; it is enormous.

Encoding: meaning makes memories

Not all encoding is equal. In a classic paper, psychologists Fergus Craik and Robert Lockhart argued that the deeper you process information, by thinking about what it means and how it connects, the better you tend to remember it (Craik & Lockhart, 1972). Shallow processing, like noticing what a word looks like or copying it, leaves a weak trace. Deep processing, like asking why something is true, leaves a stronger one.

Compare two ways of studying the word keratinocyte:

  • Shallow: Copy "keratinocyte: most common cell in epidermis" five times.
  • Deep: Notice it is keratin plus -cyte, so it is a cell that makes keratin. Ask why the skin needs keratin (it is a tough protein that makes the surface strong and helps keep water in). Connect it to fingernails and hair, which are also made of keratin.

The deep version takes a little more effort, but it ties the new word to things you already know. Those ties are like extra paths to the same memory. On an exam, any one of those paths can lead you back to the answer.

This is why the strategies in Chapters 3 and 4 work. Self-explaining, paraphrasing, building concept maps and drawing flow charts are all ways to process material deeply. They force you to think about meaning, not just surface.

Storage: memories need time and sleep

A memory is not finished the moment you learn something. For hours and days afterward, the brain keeps working to stabilize new memories and link them with older ones. This process is called consolidation. A large body of research suggests that sleep plays an important role in it (Diekelmann & Born, 2010). That is one more reason why staying up all night before an exam is a poor trade: you cut short the very process that locks in what you studied. Chapter 8 talks more about sleep the night before an exam, and Chapter 12 covers sleep habits in general.

Storage also benefits from coming back to material more than once with time in between. Each time you return and pull the material out of memory again, the memory gets stronger and more lasting. That idea leads straight to the most famous graph in the science of learning.

The forgetting curve

In the 1880s, the German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus ran a long series of memory experiments on himself. He memorized lists of nonsense syllables, then tested how much he still remembered after different amounts of time. He found a striking pattern: forgetting is fast at first and then slows down. Much of what he learned was lost within the first day, and the losses after that were smaller (Ebbinghaus, 1885/1913). More than a century later, researchers repeated his experiment with modern methods and found a very similar curve (Murre & Dros, 2015).

Your A&P terms are not nonsense syllables, and meaningful material is usually remembered better. But the basic shape holds for most things we learn: without review, memory fades quickly. Figure 5.2 sketches that idea, along with what happens when you review.

What the curve means for BIO 243

Look at the green line in Figure 5.2. After each review, memory comes back up, and then it drops more slowly than before. That is the pattern that spaced review creates. A few short reviews, spread out over days and weeks, can keep material available for a long time. That matters a lot in a course with a cumulative final. Material from OpenStax Chapter 3 that you review a few times across the term will still be with you at the final. Material you studied once, the night before Exam 1, will mostly be gone.

Most students are surprised by how much has already faded, even after only a day or two. That gap is the forgetting curve in action, and the two minutes you just spent recalling were also a review that will help you remember more of it next time.

Retrieval: the act of remembering changes memory

Here is one of the most important and surprising findings in the science of learning: pulling information out of memory does not just measure learning, it causes learning. Each time you successfully retrieve something, you make it easier to retrieve again later. This is called the testing effect or retrieval practice.

In one well-known experiment, college students learned foreign-language word pairs. Students who kept testing themselves on the words remembered far more a week later than students who kept restudying them, even though the restudying students spent their time looking at the right answers (Karpicke & Roediger, 2008). Chapter 6 covers retrieval practice in detail, because it is the single best-supported study strategy you can use.

Recognition is not recall

Some students think that because BIO 243 exams are multiple choice, they only need to recognize the right answer when they see it. That is a trap. Good multiple-choice questions have wrong answers that look very believable. If the question asks which cell breaks down bone, the options might be osteoblast, osteoclast, osteocyte and chondrocyte. If you have only a vague feeling of familiarity with those words, they all look possible. If you can recall clearly that -clast means breaker and osteoclasts resorb bone, the answer jumps out. Study as if every exam were a fill-in-the-blank test, and the multiple-choice exam will feel easier.

The fluency illusion: why rereading fools you

If retrieval is so powerful, why do so many students spend most of their time rereading and highlighting? Because those methods feel great. When you read a chapter for the third time, everything looks familiar. The words flow. You think, "I know this." That smooth, easy feeling is called fluency, and it is misleading. It tells you the material is familiar, not that you can recall it or use it on a new question.

Researchers who study how students learn have described this as one of the main illusions in self-regulated learning: we tend to judge our learning by how easy studying feels, but the methods that feel easy often produce less lasting learning, while methods that feel harder often produce more (Bjork et al., 2013). Robert Bjork and colleagues call these helpful kinds of effort desirable difficulties.

A major review that rated ten common study techniques reached a similar conclusion. It rated practice testing and spaced practice as having high usefulness, and it rated rereading and highlighting as having low usefulness (Dunlosky et al., 2013). Figure 5.3 shows how that plays out.

Picture this

Imagine two students, Priya and Sam, studying OpenStax Chapter 6 for Exam 2. Priya reads the chapter three times with a highlighter. By the third pass she feels confident: every term looks familiar. Sam reads it once, then spends the rest of his time closing the book and trying to draw a long bone from memory, labeling the diaphysis, epiphysis, periosteum and medullary cavity, and explaining out loud how osteoblasts and osteoclasts remodel bone. After a third reread, the whole chapter on bone tissue feels familiar to you. What is the best way to find out whether you really know it?

  1. Close the book and try to explain or draw the main ideas from memory.
  2. Trust the feeling, since familiarity means you have learned it.
  3. Read it a fourth time to be sure.
  4. Highlight anything that still looks unfamiliar.

Familiarity is the fluency illusion: it tells you that you have seen the material, not that you can recall and use it. Testing yourself from memory is the honest check, and it also strengthens the memory.

Three memory myths to drop

Myth or fact?

"I'm a visual learner, so I should only study with pictures."

Myth. The idea that each person has a learning style (visual, auditory, kinesthetic) and learns best when teaching matches it is very popular, but careful reviews have found no good evidence that matching instruction to a person's preferred style improves learning (Pashler et al., 2008). People do have preferences, but preferences are not the same as how we learn best. What works is matching the method to the material: anatomy is visual, so everyone should study it with diagrams; terms need to be spoken and spelled, so everyone should say and write them. Combining words and pictures helps nearly all learners.

Myth: "We only use 10 percent of our brain"

This one shows up in movies, but it is false. Brain imaging shows activity across virtually all regions of the brain over the course of a day, and damage to almost any area causes some kind of problem. You will see why in OpenStax Chapter 13, where each region of the brain has its own jobs. There is no unused 90 percent waiting to be unlocked. The good news is you do not need it. The brain you have is fully able to learn A&P with the right strategies.

Myth: "I'm good at multitasking"

Most of what feels like multitasking is actually fast switching between tasks, and each switch has a cost in time and accuracy (Rubinstein et al., 2001). In one study, people who frequently used many media at once actually did worse on a task-switching test and were more easily distracted than people who multitasked less (Ophir et al., 2009). If you study with your phone buzzing, a show playing and a group chat open, your small working memory is constantly being pulled away from the A&P. Single-tasking for 25 to 50 minutes, then taking a real break, gets more done.

Smart AI move

Put the testing effect to work with an AI tutor. After you study a section, try: "I just studied the layers of the epidermis in my Anatomy and Physiology course. Ask me to name them in order from deep to superficial and describe each one. Do not show me the answers until I try. Then tell me what I got wrong." Use it for practice only, never on graded work. And watch for a classic AI slip: some answers mix up the order of the layers or say every area of skin has a stratum lucidum. Check against OpenStax Chapter 5. The textbook wins.

A classmate says, "I'm an auditory learner, so I only listen to recorded lectures and never draw diagrams." What does the research suggest?

  1. That is the right plan, because matching your learning style improves results.
  2. Auditory learners should avoid the textbook entirely.
  3. Learning styles are real, but only for A&P.
  4. There is little evidence for matching study to a learning style; anatomy is best learned with diagrams and self-testing for everyone.

Reviews have found no solid evidence that matching instruction to a preferred learning style helps. The best method depends on the material. Visual structures like bones and neurons are best learned with diagrams and drawing from memory, whatever your preference.

Putting memory science to work

Everything in this chapter points to a handful of practical habits. You have met some already, and Chapter 6 will show you how to use them in detail:

  1. Protect working memory. Read in chunks, remove distractions, and build understanding so many facts become one chunk.
  2. Encode for meaning. Ask why, use word roots, connect structure to function, and draw.
  3. Review early and often. Reconcile notes the same day, then come back to the material a few days later and again the next week.
  4. Practice retrieval. Close the book and recall. Use flashcards, cue questions, blank diagrams and practice questions.
  5. Distrust the easy feeling. If studying feels effortless, check yourself with a test. If it feels hard but you are recalling, you are probably learning.
  6. Sleep. Give your brain the time it needs to consolidate what you studied.

None of these require more hours than you already planned in Chapter 2. They just ask you to spend those hours differently. That shift, from making material look familiar to making it retrievable, is the biggest single change most students can make in BIO 243.

  3. Look at your weekly template and add one short review of last week's chapter a few days from now.

Chapter 6

The Big Study Strategies That Work

Retrieval, spacing, mixing, and the tools that make them easy

Some study habits feel great and do very little. Others feel slow and a bit uncomfortable, and they are the ones that move your exam scores. This chapter is your toolkit of the strategies with the strongest research behind them, each one shown on real BIO 243 content.

Why the best strategies feel harder

In Chapter 5, How Memory Actually Works, you saw that rereading and highlighting feel productive because the page looks familiar. Familiar is not the same as remembered. When you read about the four basic tissue types for the third time, the words slide by easily, and your brain mistakes that ease for knowing. Then the exam asks you to name the tissue that lines the air sacs of the lungs, and the page is not there to help you.

The psychologist Robert Bjork has a name for the kind of effort that pays off later: desirable difficulties. These are study conditions that make learning feel slower and harder in the moment but lead to better long-term memory. Pulling an answer out of your own head is harder than reading it. Coming back to a topic after a few days, when it is a little fuzzy, is harder than studying it again while it is fresh. Mixing different kinds of questions is harder than doing ten of the same kind in a row. In each case the struggle is the point. It is your brain doing the work that builds memory.

That does not mean any struggle is good. Being lost because you never read the chapter is not a desirable difficulty. It is just being lost. The goal is effort aimed at the right target: recalling, connecting, and telling things apart. Here are the strategies that do that.

Retrieval practice: test yourself to learn

Retrieval practice means pulling information out of your memory on purpose, without looking. Most people think of a test as a way to measure learning. The research shows a test is also a way to cause learning. Each time you dig an answer out of your memory, you make that memory easier to find the next time.

A well-known study by Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke (2006) shows the idea clearly. College students read short science passages. Some students then read the passages again. Others took a recall test, writing down everything they could remember, without the passage in front of them. On a test given just five minutes later, rereading did slightly better. But on tests given two days and one week later, the students who had practiced recall remembered noticeably more. In one of their experiments, the students who studied over and over also felt more confident, even though they remembered less a week later. Many later studies in classrooms and labs have found the same general pattern, and a large review of study techniques by John Dunlosky and his colleagues (2013) rated practice testing as one of only two strategies with high usefulness.

You do not need fancy tools. You need to close the book and try.

Ways to practice retrieval in BIO 243

  • The blank page. After a reading session, close OpenStax and write everything you remember about the section. Then open the book and check. Fill the gaps in a different color so you can see what you missed.
  • Headings as questions. Turn each heading into a question (Chapter 3, Reading OpenStax Like a Scientist, shows how) and answer it out loud before you reread.
  • OpenStax review questions. Every chapter ends with Review Questions and Critical Thinking Questions. Answer them with the book closed, then check.
  • Flashcards, done the right way (more on that below).
  • Your own questions. The syllabus asks you to write your own test questions. Writing a good question forces you to decide what matters, and answering a stack of them a few days later is retrieval practice.

Graded work like Guided Practice and the Reading Guide also makes you retrieve and apply ideas, and that helps. Just remember these count for your grade, so do them on your own, with your notes and the textbook as the course allows, and never with AI. Your private practice is where you can quiz yourself freely.

A&P in action

Say you just read the Chapter 3 section on cell division. Instead of rereading, take a blank sheet and try to list the phases of mitosis in order and write one thing that happens in each. A strong answer looks something like this: prophase (chromosomes condense and the spindle starts to form), prometaphase (the nuclear envelope breaks down and spindle fibers attach to the chromosomes), metaphase (chromosomes line up at the middle of the cell), anaphase (sister chromatids are pulled to opposite poles), and telophase (new nuclear envelopes form), followed by cytokinesis (the cytoplasm divides). If you mixed up anaphase and metaphase, you just found out now, not on Exam 1. That gap is exactly what to study next.

In the Roediger and Karpicke (2006) study, which group remembered more on the test given one week later?

  1. Students who reread the passage several times
  2. Students who practiced recalling the passage without looking
  3. Both groups remembered the same amount
  4. Students who highlighted the passage

Rereading did slightly better on a test right away, but the recall group remembered more after two days and after a week. Retrieval practice pays off over time, which is when exams happen.

Spacing: the same hours, spread out

Spacing means spreading your study of a topic across several sessions on different days instead of packing it into one long session. The opposite is massed practice, which most of us call cramming.

The spacing effect is one of the oldest and most repeated findings in memory research. Nicholas Cepeda and his colleagues (2006) pulled together the results of hundreds of experiments on spaced versus massed practice. Across them, spreading out practice led to better memory on later tests than massing it, for the same total study time. They also found that the best gap between sessions depends on how long you need to remember the material. If you need it for weeks, your review sessions should be spread across days, not minutes.

Why would spacing help? One useful way to think about it: when you come back to a topic after a day or two, it has faded a little. Pulling it back up takes effort, and that effort strengthens the memory, much like retrieval practice. When you study the same thing again ten minutes later, it is still sitting in your head, so the second pass adds very little.

What spacing looks like in a real week

The syllabus asks for 4 to 6 hours a week of study for lecture. That is not meant as one heroic Sunday. Think of it as four to six short sessions spread over the week, each one 30 to 60 minutes. Chapter 2, Your Real Week, walks you through building that schedule around work, commuting and family.

Inside each session, start with a few minutes of review of older material before you move to the new chapter. If you are on Chapter 6 bone tissue in week 6, spend the first ten minutes quizzing yourself on Chapter 5, the integumentary system. Those small look-backs are spacing built right into your routine, and they matter even more because the final exam is cumulative.

Myth or fact?

"Cramming works for me. I always got good grades that way in high school."

Partly true, and that is why it is a trap. Cramming can get you through a test the next morning, because the material is still fresh. The research problem is what happens after that: crammed material fades fast. In BIO 243 that matters twice. First, each unit builds on the last, so muscle contraction in Chapter 10 leans on the cell membrane ideas from Chapter 3, and nervous tissue in Chapter 12 leans on both. Second, the final exam is cumulative. Anything you crammed for Exam 1 you will have to learn again for the final. Spacing is not only better learning. It is less total work across the semester.

Interleaving: mix it up

Most textbooks and homework sets are blocked. You do ten questions on epithelial tissue, then ten on connective tissue, then ten on muscle tissue. Blocked practice feels smooth, because you already know what kind of answer each question wants. Interleaving means shuffling those question types together, so you have to figure out what kind of problem you are facing before you can solve it.

Doug Rohrer and Kelli Taylor (2007) tested this with college students learning to find the volumes of different geometric solids. One group practiced the problem types in blocks. Another group practiced the same problems shuffled. During practice, the shuffled group actually did worse. But on a test one week later, the shuffled group did much better. A separate study by Nate Kornell and Robert Bjork (2008) found a similar benefit when people learned to recognize the painting styles of different artists from mixed examples instead of one artist at a time. The research on interleaving is younger and smaller than the research on testing and spacing, and it seems to help most when the things being mixed are easy to confuse. That describes a lot of A&P.

Think about how Exam 1 really works. It does not announce "the next five questions are about connective tissue." It shows you a description, such as a tissue with cells sitting in lacunae inside a firm, glassy matrix, and you have to decide that it is hyaline cartilage before you can answer. Blocked practice never trains that decision. Interleaving does.

To interleave, you do not need a special tool. Shuffle your flashcards across chapters. When you write your own practice questions, mix them into one pile. When you review for Exam 2, do not study Chapter 5, then 6, then 9, then 10 in neat order every time. After the first pass, mix them.

Try it now

Without looking, sort each of these into one of the four tissue types: epithelial, connective, muscle, or nervous.

  1. Blood
  2. The lining of the small intestine
  3. The wall of the heart that contracts
  4. Tissue that carries signals from the spinal cord to a muscle
  5. Adipose (fat) tissue
  6. The outer layer of the skin

Answers: 1 connective (yes, blood is a connective tissue with a liquid matrix), 2 epithelial, 3 muscle (cardiac), 4 nervous, 5 connective, 6 epithelial (stratified squamous). If blood or fat surprised you, that is the value of mixing: it caught a confusion that blocked practice would have hidden.

Elaboration: ask why and how

A fact on its own is easy to forget. A fact tied to a reason is much easier to keep. Elaboration means connecting new information to things you already know, usually by asking "why is this true?" and "how does this work?" The Dunlosky (2013) review rated elaborative questioning and self-explanation as moderately useful, which is a solid result for a free strategy you can use anywhere.

Here is elaboration on a fact from Chapter 4. The fact: the walls of the alveoli in the lungs are made of simple squamous epithelium. Now ask why. Simple means one layer. Squamous means flat cells. Gases have to cross that wall to get into and out of the blood, and a single layer of flat cells gives the shortest possible path. So the structure fits the job. Once you have the "because," you can predict other places this tissue should show up, like the lining of capillaries, where nutrients and gases also have to cross quickly. That ability to predict is what turns memorizing into understanding.

Self-explanation

Self-explanation is a close cousin. As you read a passage or work through a figure, you stop and explain to yourself what each step means and how it connects to the step before. In a classic study, Michelene Chi and her colleagues (1989) found that the stronger physics students explained worked examples to themselves line by line, while weaker students tended to read them without explaining. In A&P, try it with any process figure. Look at the OpenStax figure of bone remodeling and say, out loud, "Osteoclasts break down old bone matrix, which releases calcium into the blood. Osteoblasts lay down new matrix. When an osteoblast gets trapped in the matrix it made, it becomes an osteocyte that maintains the bone." If you cannot explain a step, that is the step to reread.

Concrete examples

Abstract categories are slippery. Concrete examples give you something to hold. When you learn the six types of synovial joints, attach a real example to each, ideally one you can move on your own body:

  • Hinge: the elbow (between the humerus and the ulna). It bends and straightens in one plane.
  • Pivot: the joint between the first and second neck vertebrae (atlas and axis), which lets you shake your head "no."
  • Ball-and-socket: the shoulder and the hip, which move in many directions.
  • Saddle: the base of the thumb, which is why your thumb can cross your palm.
  • Condyloid: the knuckles where fingers meet the hand, and the wrist joint.
  • Plane: the small gliding joints between the carpal bones of the wrist.

Even better, use two examples per category and ask what they have in common. That pushes you from "the elbow is a hinge" toward "a hinge joint has a curved surface fitting into a trough, so it only moves in one plane."

Dual coding: words plus pictures

Dual coding means learning an idea in both words and a picture, then linking the two. The idea comes from Allan Paivio's work on how the mind stores verbal and visual information. For you, it means: when you study the sarcomere, do not just read "the I band and H zone shorten during contraction while the A band stays the same length." Draw the sarcomere relaxed, then draw it contracted, and label what changed. Your drawing and your sentence now support each other.

This is not the same as learning styles. Dual coding is good for everyone, because pictures plus words beat words alone for most people and most content. The claim that some people are "visual learners" who learn best only from pictures has not held up when it is properly tested. Chapter 5 covers that myth in more detail, and Chapter 7, Studying Structures, Diagrams and Processes, will show you how to draw your way through A&P.

Which question is the best example of elaboration while studying bone tissue?

  1. "What color is the highlighter I used on osteoblasts?"
  2. "How many times have I read this section?"
  3. "Why would a bone need cells that break down matrix as well as cells that build it?"
  4. "What page number is the osteon figure on?"

Elaboration asks why and how. Answering this question links osteoclasts and osteoblasts to remodeling, repair and calcium balance, which makes both cell types easier to remember and harder to confuse.

Flashcards done right

Flashcards are one of the easiest ways to do retrieval practice and spacing at the same time. They are also easy to do badly. Flipping a card, glancing at the answer, and thinking "yeah, I knew that" is just rereading in a smaller font. Here is how to make flashcards actually work.

  1. Say or write the answer before you flip. Out loud, under your breath, or on scrap paper. If you did not commit to an answer, you did not test yourself.
  2. Study in both directions. Do "osteoclast" to "a cell that breaks down bone matrix." Then do "a cell that breaks down bone matrix" to "osteoclast." Exams ask both ways, and knowing a term when you see it is easier than producing it from a description.
  3. Keep the cards you miss. Do not drop a card just because you got it right once. A card is learned when you get it right on a later day, not twice in a row in the same session.
  4. Make cards that carry ideas, not just words. "Where is calcium stored in a skeletal muscle fiber, and what releases it?" (the sarcoplasmic reticulum; an action potential traveling down the T-tubules triggers the release) is better than a card that just says "sarcoplasmic reticulum."
  5. Shuffle across chapters once you have learned the basics. That is interleaving for free.
  6. Use one bigger stack, not many tiny ones. Nate Kornell (2009) found that studying vocabulary cards in one large stack, which naturally spaces out the repetitions of each card, led to better learning than studying the same cards in several small stacks. Most of his participants believed the small stacks worked better. Your feelings about what works are not always a reliable guide.

The Leitner box system

A simple way to run your flashcards is the Leitner system, named after Sebastian Leitner, who described it decades ago. You can use real boxes, envelopes, rubber bands, or the folders in a flashcard app.

All new cards start in Box 1. Box 1 gets reviewed every session. When you answer a card correctly, it moves up one box. Box 2 gets reviewed every other session, and Box 3 about once a week. Any card you miss, from any box, goes back to Box 1. The result is that the hard cards, like the difference between osteoblasts and osteoclasts or between synarthrosis and diarthrosis, show up again and again, while the cards you truly know only drop by now and then to stay fresh.

CircleIn flashcards

BIO 243 uses CircleIn, a graded study-community app paid for by USC Upstate. You can make flashcard decks there, share notes, and answer classmates' questions. Two tips. First, make your own cards before you study someone else's deck. The act of choosing what goes on a card, and putting it in your own words, is part of the learning. Second, when you use a classmate's deck, apply the same rules: answer before flipping, go both directions, and keep the ones you miss. Because CircleIn posts count toward your grade, write them yourself. AI should not write your CircleIn cards or posts.

Smart AI move

AI cannot make the effort of remembering for you, but it can be a patient quiz partner. Try a prompt like this: "I'm studying the six types of synovial joints for an A&P class. Give me one description at a time of a joint movement, and I will name the joint type and an example. Don't give me the answer until I respond. Mix the order." After the session, check any answer you were unsure about against OpenStax Chapter 9. AI makes confident mistakes, and the textbook wins. Never paste in Guided Practice, Reading Guide, quiz or exam questions. Those are graded work, and the BIO 243 AI rules say no AI there. Chapter 11 has many more prompts like this.

Practice questions and teaching a friend

Practice questions

Practice questions are retrieval practice in the same format as your exam. BIO 243 exams are multiple choice, so practicing with multiple-choice questions helps you get used to reading carefully and telling close answers apart. Good sources include the OpenStax Review Questions at the end of each chapter, Dr. Garg's free BIO 243 study-tools site, the questions you write yourself, and the ones your study group writes for each other.

Make practice questions count. Answer each one before looking at the choices when you can, then pick the choice that matches your answer. After you check, do not just note "right" or "wrong." For each miss, write a short line about why: "I mixed up the A band and I band," or "I didn't know that blood is a connective tissue." Those notes become your next study session.

Teaching a friend

Explaining a topic to someone else forces you to organize it, put it in your own words, and notice where your understanding has holes. Logan Fiorella and Richard Mayer (2013) found that students who prepared to teach a lesson and then actually explained it on video did better on a later test than students who only studied, and also better than students who only prepared to teach. The effect sizes in studies like this vary, so treat it as a useful tool rather than a magic one.

You do not need a willing friend. Explain the action potential to your little brother, your dog, your car's steering wheel on the commute, or your phone's voice recorder. Then play it back. Where did you stall? Where did you say "and then it, um, does the thing"? That is your study list.

Studying with classmates is a wonderful way to practice explaining. Just keep the line the syllabus draws: study together, but answer graded work alone.

Putting it together: one study session

You do not have to pick one strategy. The best sessions combine several. Here is a sample 45-minute session for week 6, when you are learning Chapter 6 bone tissue after Exam 1:

  1. Look back (5 minutes). Quiz yourself on Chapter 5 with Box 1 flashcards. That is spacing and retrieval.
  2. Learn something new (15 minutes). Read the OpenStax section on bone cells. Stop after each paragraph and explain it to yourself. That is self-explanation.
  3. Draw it (10 minutes). Close the book. Draw an osteon from memory and label the central canal, lamellae, lacunae, osteocytes and canaliculi. Check and fix. That is dual coding plus retrieval.
  4. Ask why (5 minutes). Write two "why" questions and answer them, like "Why do osteocytes need canaliculi?" (Because the matrix is hard and mineralized, so nutrients and wastes have to travel through those tiny channels to reach cells trapped in it.)
  5. Make new cards (10 minutes). Turn today's learning into flashcards, both directions, and put them in Box 1.

Notice what is missing: rereading the whole section, highlighting, and recopying slides. Those feel like work, but the strategies above are where your time pays off.

Chapter 7

Studying Structures, Diagrams and Processes

How to learn the parts, the pictures and the steps

A&P asks you to learn two very different things: the parts of the body and the processes that make those parts work. Each one needs its own study tools. This chapter shows you how to learn a structure so it sticks, how to practice with diagrams and models, and how to turn a long process into a story you can tell from memory.

Two kinds of A&P knowledge

Look at any OpenStax chapter in BIO 243 and you will find two kinds of content mixed together.

Structures are the "what" and "where" of the body. The parts of a long bone. The layers of the epidermis. The lobes of the cerebrum. The twelve cranial nerves. Learning structures is a lot like learning a map: names, locations, and what sits next to what.

Processes are the "how" and "why." How a bone grows in length. How a muscle fiber contracts. How a neuron fires an action potential. How the sympathetic nervous system speeds up your heart. Learning a process is like learning a recipe or the plot of a movie: steps in order, where each step causes the next.

Many students use one method for everything, usually reading and flashcards. Flashcards are great for names, but they chop a process into separate facts, and you lose the order and the cause. Long reading sessions can explain a process, but they do not train you to find structures on a model or a slide. The rest of this chapter gives you a tool kit for each kind, and they all build on the strategies from Chapter 6: retrieval practice, spacing, elaboration and dual coding.

This chapter belongs to week 7 for a reason. Around then you are deep in Chapter 10, muscle tissue, which holds one of the longest processes in the course, and just ahead are nervous tissue and the central nervous system, which are full of both structures and processes. Getting your methods right now pays off through Exam 3 and the final.

Learning a structure: the four questions

When you meet a new structure, do not just memorize its name. Ask four questions about it. Each answer gives your memory another hook to hang the name on.

  1. Where is it? Use the directional terms from Chapter 1: superior and inferior, medial and lateral, proximal and distal, anterior and posterior. "Somewhere in the arm" is not enough. "The lateral bone of the forearm" is.
  2. What are its neighbors? What does it touch, connect to, or sit beside? Anatomy is a neighborhood. If you know the neighbors, you can find the house.
  3. What does it do? Function is the strongest hook of all, because it gives the structure a reason to exist. A structure with a job is much easier to remember than a word with a location.
  4. What does its name mean? Most anatomical names are Latin or Greek descriptions. Once you decode the name, it often tells you the shape, the location, or the job.

Notice what happened in Figure 7.1. Your BIO 243 chapters do not cover the radius in detail (that is mostly Chapter 8 in OpenStax, the appendicular skeleton, and BIO 243 touches it through joints in Chapter 9). But the method works on anything. Try it on a muscle name you meet in lab: the sternocleidomastoid. The name looks terrifying until you split it. Sterno means sternum, cleido means clavicle, and mastoid means the mastoid process of the temporal bone, the bump behind your ear. The name is a map: the muscle runs from the sternum and clavicle up to the mastoid process. Its job is to turn and flex the head. And here is a bonus neighbor: it is controlled by cranial nerve XI, the accessory nerve, which you will meet in Chapter 13.

Word parts do a lot of the work

Chapter 3 suggested keeping a running vocabulary list with word roots. In anatomy, those roots pay off constantly. A few that run through Exam 2:

  • osteo- means bone. chondro- means cartilage. myo- or sarco- means muscle.
  • -blast means a building or growing cell. -clast means a breaking cell. -cyte means a mature cell.
  • Put them together: an osteoblast builds bone, an osteoclast breaks bone down, and an osteocyte is a mature bone cell maintaining the matrix. A chondrocyte is a mature cartilage cell.
  • epi- means upon or above, dia- means through or across, and peri- means around. So the periosteum is the membrane around the bone, and the epiphysis sits on the end of the long bone's shaft, the diaphysis.

A&P in action

Run the four questions on the epiphyseal plate from Chapter 6, bone tissue.

Where? In a growing long bone, between the diaphysis (the shaft) and the epiphysis (the end), in the region called the metaphysis. Neighbors? The diaphysis on one side, the epiphysis on the other, and the bone marrow and blood supply nearby. Job? It is a layer of hyaline cartilage where chondrocytes keep dividing, which lets the bone grow in length. The cartilage on the diaphysis side is gradually replaced by bone. Name? Epi (upon) plus physis (growth): the growth zone near the epiphysis. And an elaboration bonus: when growth ends in early adulthood, the cartilage is fully replaced by bone and leaves an epiphyseal line. That is why an X-ray can hint at whether someone's bones are still growing.

Blank-diagram practice

A&P figures carry a huge amount of the content. The single best way to learn a labeled figure is not to stare at it. It is to cover the labels and try to fill them in. That is retrieval practice applied to pictures.

How to make a blank diagram

  • Cover the labels on the OpenStax figure with small sticky notes or strips of paper.
  • Screenshot the figure and draw white boxes over the labels with any photo or drawing app.
  • Print the figure and use correction tape over the labels.
  • Use the unlabeled images and virtual labs on Dr. Garg's free BIO 243 study-tools site.

Climb the ladder

Do not stop at one level. Move up a ladder of difficulty as the easy level starts to feel easy:

  1. Label with a word bank. Write the terms in a list on the side, then match them to the blanks. This is the starting point, good for the first day.
  2. Label with no word bank. Now you must produce every name yourself, and spell it. Exams and practicals will not give you a list.
  3. Draw it from scratch. A blank page, no figure. Sketch the structure and label it. Your drawing can be rough; the labels and their positions are what count.
  4. Explain it. Point to each part and say what it does. Now you are linking structure to function, which is what higher-level exam questions ask for.

After each attempt, check against the real figure and mark your misses in a different color. Return to the same diagram a day or two later. The misses you circled are your first target. That is spacing, built right in.

You can label the parts of an osteon perfectly when the word bank is on the page. What is the best next step?

  1. Keep using the word bank, since it is working
  2. Reread the osteon section of the textbook three times
  3. Highlight the labels on the original figure
  4. Label the osteon with no word bank, then draw it from scratch

Matching words to blanks is recognition, which is easier than recall. Moving up the ladder to labeling with no word bank, and then drawing from memory, trains the harder skill that exams and practicals require.

Models, slides and lab practicals

Lab is part of your BIO 243 grade, and lab asks you to find structures on real objects: plastic models, bones, microscope slides and images. That is harder than finding them on a clean textbook figure, for a few reasons. A model may be a different color than the book. It may be turned around, cut at a different angle, or show the left side when you studied the right. A microscope slide never looks quite like the photo in the book.

A few habits help a lot:

  • Study from more than one view. If you only ever see a femur from the front, a back view will throw you. Rotate the model. Look at the bone from every side.
  • Learn features, not pictures. Instead of memorizing what one image looks like, learn the features that identify a structure. Then you can recognize it from any angle.
  • Use your own body. You carry a skeleton everywhere. Feel the olecranon of your ulna (the point of your elbow), the mastoid process behind your ear, the spine of your scapula, and the medial and lateral malleoli at your ankle. Bend and turn your joints and name the movement: flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, rotation, supination, pronation.
  • Practice under practical-like conditions. If your lab uses timed practicals, practice the same way. Have a friend point to structures and give you a short time to name each one, or use a timed quiz on the study-tools site.
  • Use open lab time and virtual labs. Seeing the model more than once, on more than one day, beats one long session the night before.

Try it now

Learning features, not pictures, matters most for histology. Read each description and name the type of muscle tissue: skeletal, cardiac or smooth.

  1. Long, cylindrical cells with stripes (striations) and many nuclei pushed to the edge of the cell.
  2. Spindle-shaped cells with no visible striations and one central nucleus.
  3. Striated, branching cells joined end to end by dark lines called intercalated discs, usually with one central nucleus.

Answers: 1 skeletal, 2 smooth, 3 cardiac. Now make this a feature checklist you can use on any slide: Striated or not? Branched or not? How many nuclei, and where? Intercalated discs? Four questions will identify all three tissues from any angle.

Processes as stories

Processes are where many BIO 243 students lose the most points. The usual mistake is to memorize a process as a list of separate facts. "Calcium. Troponin. Tropomyosin. Myosin. ATP." Each word is right, but under exam pressure the list falls apart, because nothing holds the pieces in order.

The fix is to learn a process as a story: a series of steps where each step happens because of the one before it. A story has a beginning, an order, and causes. If you know why step 4 happens, you cannot forget that step 3 came first. When you write a process out, put the word "so" or "because" between every step. If you cannot fill in the "because," you have found the part you do not understand yet.

The muscle contraction story

Here is excitation-contraction coupling and the sliding filament model from Chapter 10, told as one story. Figure 7.2 shows the same story as a flow chart.

Now read it as a story with "because" links. A signal comes down the motor neuron, so the axon terminal releases acetylcholine. Acetylcholine binds to receptors on the muscle fiber, so sodium rushes in and starts an action potential in the muscle cell. That action potential races along the sarcolemma and dives deep into the fiber through the T-tubules, so the sarcoplasmic reticulum lets go of its stored calcium. Calcium binds to troponin, so tropomyosin is pulled off the binding sites on actin. Now the myosin heads, already cocked by splitting ATP, can grab actin and pull, so the thin filaments slide toward the center of the sarcomere. A new ATP binds each myosin head so it can let go, re-cock, and grab again. As long as calcium and ATP are there, the cycle repeats.

Once you have the story, you can answer the "what if" questions that exams love, because you can reason through them instead of hoping you memorized them:

  • What if acetylcholinesterase is blocked? ACh is not cleared from the synapse, so the muscle keeps getting stimulated and has trouble relaxing.
  • What if there is no ATP, as after death? Myosin heads cannot detach from actin, so the muscle stays locked. That is rigor mortis.
  • What if calcium stays in the sarcoplasm? Troponin keeps tropomyosin pulled aside, so cross-bridges keep forming and the muscle stays contracted.
  • What happens to the bands of the sarcomere? The Z discs move closer together, so the I band and H zone shrink, but the A band, which is the length of the thick filaments, stays the same.

The action potential story

The action potential in Chapter 12 is the other great process of the course, and it shows up again in muscle, in the senses, and in the autonomic nervous system. It is easiest to learn as a story told along a graph, because the graph shows you the membrane voltage at each moment.

Tell it with "because" links. At rest, the inside of the neuron sits around -70 millivolts. A stimulus nudges it upward. If it reaches threshold, about -55 mV, voltage-gated sodium channels open, so sodium rushes in, because there is much more sodium outside the cell and the inside is negative. That inrush makes the inside positive, up to about +30 mV. At the peak, the sodium channels inactivate and voltage-gated potassium channels open, so potassium flows out and the voltage falls back down. The potassium channels are slow to close, so the voltage briefly dips below resting, which is called hyperpolarization. Then the cell settles back to rest, while the sodium-potassium pump keeps the gradients in place over the long run.

The muscle story and the neuron story connect. Step 1 of Figure 7.2 is the whole of Figure 7.3, arriving at an axon terminal. When you see that link, two big topics become one bigger, easier-to-remember story.

During an action potential, what causes the membrane to move from about -55 mV up to about +30 mV?

  1. Potassium ions flowing out of the cell
  2. Sodium ions rushing into the cell through voltage-gated channels
  3. The sodium-potassium pump moving three sodium ions out
  4. Calcium being released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum

Once threshold is reached, voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open and positive sodium ions rush in, driving the rapid depolarization. Potassium leaving is what brings the voltage back down afterward.

Drawing from memory

You do not have to be an artist to learn by drawing. Researchers have found that drawing a word or idea tends to improve later memory for it compared with simply writing it out, an effect Jeffrey Wammes and his colleagues (2016) called the drawing effect. A review by Peggy Van Meter and Joanna Garner (2005) found that drawing your own diagrams while you learn can help, especially when you get some guidance on what to draw and then check your drawing against an accurate source. In other words, drawing works best as retrieval plus feedback, not as decoration.

Some drawings worth doing from memory in BIO 243:

  • A long bone with diaphysis, epiphyses, articular cartilage, periosteum, endosteum, medullary cavity, and epiphyseal plate or line.
  • An osteon with its central canal, rings of lamellae, lacunae holding osteocytes, and canaliculi linking them.
  • A synovial joint with the articular capsule, synovial membrane, joint cavity filled with synovial fluid, and articular cartilage.
  • A sarcomere, relaxed and contracted, side by side, with Z discs, M line, thick and thin filaments, A band, I band and H zone.
  • A neuron with dendrites, cell body, axon hillock, axon, myelin sheath, nodes of Ranvier and axon terminals, plus an arrow showing which way the signal travels.
  • A chemical synapse, showing the presynaptic terminal, vesicles, cleft, receptors on the postsynaptic cell, and the steps in order.

Keep your drawings simple: boxes, circles and lines. Time yourself. A two-minute sketch from memory, checked against the book, is worth more than a twenty-minute copy of the figure.

Mnemonics: use them sparingly and correctly

A mnemonic is a memory trick, often a sentence where each word starts with the same letter as an item on a list. Mnemonics can be useful in A&P for one specific job: remembering a list with a fixed order that has no built-in logic. They are much less useful for understanding. The Dunlosky (2013) review rated the keyword mnemonic, one common type, as having low overall usefulness, partly because the memories it builds can fade quickly and because it works for some kinds of material much better than others.

The classic A&P case is the twelve pairs of cranial nerves in Chapter 13. They are numbered in Roman numerals, roughly from front to back along the brain, and the exam will expect you to know the name, number and job of each one.

Two common mnemonics are at the bottom of Figure 7.4. The first word of each gives you the first letter of each nerve in order: Olfactory, Optic, Oculomotor, Trochlear, Trigeminal, Abducens, Facial, Vestibulocochlear, Glossopharyngeal, Vagus, Accessory, Hypoglossal. The second gives you the type of each nerve in order: sensory, sensory, motor, motor, both, and so on.

Rules for using mnemonics well

  • Use them for order, not for meaning. The mnemonic tells you the seventh nerve starts with F. It does not tell you that the facial nerve controls the muscles of facial expression and carries taste from the front of the tongue. Learn the jobs with the four questions and with stories.
  • Know which "O" or "T" is which. Three nerves start with O and two with T. Practice saying the full names, not just the letters.
  • Pair them with meaning when you can. The abducens nerve (VI) moves the eye laterally, which is abduction, away from the midline. The name and the job match. The hypoglossal nerve (XII) runs below (hypo) the tongue (glossal) and moves it.
  • Keep them few. A handful of mnemonics for true lists is helpful. Fifty mnemonics for everything becomes its own memory burden.
  • Keep them clean. Some A&P mnemonics passed around online are crude. Make your own instead. A mnemonic you invented is usually easier to remember anyway.

Myth or fact?

"If I can recite the mnemonic, I know the cranial nerves."

Myth. Reciting the mnemonic only proves you know twelve first letters. A typical exam question will look more like this: a patient cannot move one eye laterally; which nerve is most likely damaged? The mnemonic will not answer that. You need to know that the lateral rectus muscle moves the eye laterally and that the abducens nerve, VI, controls it. Use the mnemonic as a hanger, and then hang real meaning on every hook. Chapter 16 in OpenStax, the neurological exam, shows how clinicians test each cranial nerve, which is a great way to attach a job to each name.

Smart AI move

AI can help you turn a process into a story, but A&P is a place where it makes very believable mistakes. Try a prompt like: "Explain the steps of excitation-contraction coupling in skeletal muscle as a short story, linking each step to the next with the word because. I am a pre-nursing student in my first A&P course." Then check every step against OpenStax Chapter 10, and compare it with your own version. Watch especially for mixed-up details, such as an answer saying the trochlear nerve moves the lateral rectus (it is the abducens) or that calcium binds tropomyosin (it binds troponin). Finding those errors is excellent studying in itself. As always, keep AI out of graded work, quizzes and exams.

A patient cannot turn one eye outward, away from the nose. Which cranial nerve is most likely involved?

  1. II, optic
  2. IV, trochlear
  3. VI, abducens
  4. VIII, vestibulocochlear

The abducens nerve controls the lateral rectus muscle, which abducts the eye, turning it laterally. The name even hints at the job. A mnemonic gives you the order of names but cannot answer this question; knowing the function can.

Your structure and process toolkit

When you sit down to study, first ask: is this a structure or a process? Then pick the tool.

  • For a structure: answer the four questions, decode the name, practice blank diagrams up the ladder, find it on a model or on your own body, and draw it from memory.
  • For a process: write it as a story with "so" and "because" between steps, draw it as a flow chart or a graph, ask "what if" questions, and tell it out loud without notes.
  • For a true ordered list: use a mnemonic, then attach meaning to every item.

All of these are forms of the strategies from Chapter 6. Blank diagrams are retrieval practice. Coming back to the same diagram after two days is spacing. "Because" links are elaboration. Drawing is dual coding. You are not learning a new set of tricks. You are using the same strong strategies in the shapes that A&P content comes in. Chapter 8, Plan Backward, will show you how to fit all of this into the days before an exam.

Chapter 8

Plan Backward: Exam Prep Week by Week

A timeline that starts ten days out and ends with a good night's sleep

Good exam scores in BIO 243 are rarely built in the last two nights. They are built over the ten days before the exam, one short session at a time. This chapter shows you how to start from exam day, count backward, and lay out a plan you can actually follow, even with a job, a commute or a family.

Why plan backward?

Most students plan forward. They study what feels urgent today and hope it adds up by exam day. The trouble is that "urgent" usually means "due tomorrow," so the exam keeps sliding to the bottom of the list until it suddenly is tomorrow. Planning backward flips that. You start with the exam, decide what has to be true by then, and work back to what you need to do today.

It matters a lot in BIO 243 because of how the course is built. There are three unit exams plus a cumulative final, and the syllabus is honest about it: it is very hard to recover from a low test grade, so it pays to start strong. In a typical term, Exam 1 lands around week 5 and covers Chapters 1 to 4. Exam 2 lands around week 8 and covers Chapters 5, 6, 9 and 10. Exam 3 comes around week 13 and covers Chapters 12, 13 and 16. The final comes after week 15. Check your syllabus and Blackboard for the exact timing in your section.

That is why this chapter is tied to weeks 4, 8 and 13. You will want to come back to it before each exam. The first time, use it to build your plan. The second and third times, you will be adjusting a plan you already know.

Planning backward also uses the strategies from Chapter 6 in the right shape. Ten days gives you room for spacing, so every topic gets more than one visit. It gives you time for retrieval practice and a full practice test, with days left over to fix what the practice test reveals. A two-night cram has room for none of that. In a large online study of spacing, Nicholas Cepeda and his colleagues (2008) found that the best gap between study sessions grew as the test got farther away. For a test about a week off, sessions spaced roughly a day apart worked far better than sessions minutes apart. Ten days of short sessions gives you exactly that kind of gap.

The ten-day timeline

Start your exam prep about 7 to 10 days before the exam. Ten is ideal. Seven still works if you are organized. Fewer than that, and you are mostly sorting out what you can skip. Figure 8.1 shows the four phases.

Days 10 to 8: map it

This phase is short, maybe one or two study blocks. You are not learning yet. You are making a map.

  • List what the exam covers. Write every chapter and, under each, the main topics or learning objectives your instructor has given. If there is a study guide or objectives list in Blackboard, start there.
  • Rate each topic. Use a traffic light. Green: I could explain this to a classmate right now without notes. Yellow: I sort of know it, but I would hesitate. Red: I am lost, or I have not studied it yet. Be honest. Nobody sees this list but you.
  • Gather your materials. Your rewritten notes, your flashcards, your blank diagrams, the OpenStax chapters, and the Reading Guide. Check how much of the Reading Guide you have finished.
  • Look at your real calendar. When are your work shifts, classes, family duties and commutes over the next ten days? Find your study blocks first, then assign topics to them. Chapter 2, Your Real Week, has more on fitting study around real life.

Days 7 to 4: learn and fill gaps

This is the longest phase and the heart of your prep. Each block focuses on one chapter or one big topic, starting with your red topics while you have the most time and energy. Use the strategies from Chapters 6 and 7: blank-page recall, flashcards in both directions, blank diagrams, and processes written as stories with "because" links.

The key move is to open every block with about ten minutes of mixed review: quick recall on topics from earlier blocks and earlier chapters, shuffled together. That keeps older material from fading while you work on new material, and it gives you the interleaving that Chapter 6 described.

Days 3 to 2: test yourself

Now take a full practice test, closed book, timed, covering every chapter on the exam. Then spend the next day working on what it showed you. Do not skip this phase. It is the one most likely to catch the gap that would have cost you points.

Day 1: wind down

The day before the exam is for light, targeted review, finishing and submitting the Reading Guide, getting your things ready, and sleeping. It is not the day to learn a whole chapter for the first time. The rest of this chapter covers each of these pieces in more detail.

Try it now

Take two minutes and rate the topics for your next exam. If it is Exam 1, your list might include: anatomical terms and body regions, homeostasis and feedback loops, the four tissue types and the types of epithelium, chemical bonds and pH, the parts of the cell, membrane transport, and DNA replication, mitosis and protein synthesis. Once you know your reds, yellows and greens, give each topic a share of your study blocks. Two things decide the share: how weak you are on the topic, and how big a part of the exam it is likely to be. A long, process-heavy chapter you rated red needs several blocks. A short chapter you rated green may need only mixed review.

A&P in action

On 7 days out, the plan might be: draw a sarcomere from memory, relaxed and contracted, and label the Z discs, M line, A band, I band and H zone. Check it against OpenStax. Practice tests: your most important study session

A practice test is retrieval practice in the exact form of the real thing. It does two jobs. It strengthens what you know, and it shows you clearly what you do not know while you still have time to fix it. A large meta-analysis by Olusola Adesope and his colleagues (2017) combined the results of many studies and found that practice tests generally led to better learning than restudying or other comparison activities, across many kinds of material and test formats.

How to build one

  • Collect questions from the OpenStax Review Questions at the end of each chapter, the practice items on Dr. Garg's BIO 243 study-tools site, the questions you have written yourself, and good CircleIn questions from classmates.
  • Mix them. Shuffle questions from all the exam chapters together. The real exam will not be sorted by chapter.
  • Use the format of the exam. BIO 243 exams are multiple choice, so most of your practice should be too. Add a few "explain this process" questions for the big processes, because explaining is the deepest test of all.
  • Make it real. Closed book, no phone, timed. Sit at a table, not on your bed.

How to use the results

Grade it honestly, then make an error log: for each miss, write the topic and a short reason. "Mixed up osteoblast and osteoclast." "Did not know which joints are amphiarthroses." "Read too fast and missed the word NOT." Your error log becomes the plan for the next day. Chapter 10, After the Exam, uses the same kind of sorting on the real exam afterward.

Why does the ten-day plan put the practice test 2 to 3 days before the exam instead of the night before?

  1. Practice tests are only useful when you are well rested
  2. So there is still time to study the topics the practice test shows you missed
  3. Because the Reading Guide is due the same night
  4. Practice tests lower your score if taken too close to the exam

A practice test is most valuable as a map of your gaps. Taking it a few days early leaves time to act on what it reveals, instead of just discovering the gaps the night before.

  • Do it yourself. The Reading Guide is graded work. The BIO 243 AI rules are clear: no AI on quizzes, exams or graded work, and that includes the Reading Guide. Fill it in from the textbook, in your own words where the guide asks for explanation. That is also how it turns into learning.

Once it is submitted, the completed guide is a great review sheet. On Day 1 or exam morning, cover your answers and quiz yourself from the questions.

The day before

Day 1 should feel calm, not frantic. If your plan worked, you have already done the heavy lifting. Here is what the day before can look like:

  • Short, targeted review. Go through your error log and your Box 1 flashcards. Redraw the two or three diagrams you have had the most trouble with. Explain one big process out loud. Keep it to one or two short blocks.
  • No brand-new chapters. If something is still red on Day 1, learn its main ideas, not every detail, and accept that this exam may not be perfect on that topic. Then make a note to start earlier next time.
  • Finish and submit the Reading Guide.
  • Handle logistics. Confirm when and how the exam is given in your section. If it is online in Blackboard, make sure your device is charged and your internet is reliable, and read any instructions about the exam ahead of time. If it is on campus, plan your commute with extra time for traffic and parking.
  • Clear the morning. If you can, arrange child care, trade a shift, or ask for help with family duties well before Day 1, so the morning is yours.

The night before: sleep beats the all-nighter

It is tempting to stay up late "just to get through one more chapter." For most students, most of the time, that trade loses. Sleep is not wasted study time. It is part of how memory works.

Researchers who study sleep and memory, such as Matthew Walker and Robert Stickgold (2006) and Susanne Diekelmann and Jan Born (2010), have reviewed a large body of evidence that sleep after learning helps consolidate new memories, meaning it helps stabilize them and connect them with what you already know. Sleep loss also seems to hurt the next day's learning. In one study by Seung-Schik Yoo and colleagues (2007), people who had been kept awake for a night were noticeably worse at forming new memories the next day than people who had slept normally. An exam asks your brain to retrieve and reason under pressure, and a tired brain does both less well.

There is an important nuance. A study by Kana Okano and colleagues (2019) tracked the sleep of students in a large college chemistry course with wearable devices. Students who slept more, slept better, and kept a more consistent sleep schedule over the weeks of the course tended to earn better grades. Sleep in just the night or two right before a test was not, by itself, strongly linked to scores. The study was correlational, so it cannot prove sleep caused the grades. But it fits a sensible message: one good night will not save a unit you did not study, and steady sleep all along gives you the best chance. The ten-day plan helps you do both, because you are not trying to cram the unit into the last night.

Sleep experts generally recommend that adults get 7 or more hours a night on a regular basis. If you work late shifts or care for young children, that may not always be possible. Protect what you can. Even moving your bedtime 30 minutes earlier the night before an exam is better than pulling it later.

Myth or fact?

"An all-nighter shows I'm serious, and I'll remember more because it's fresh."

Myth. Staying up all night does not show dedication to anyone, and it usually costs more than it gains. You lose the sleep that helps lock in what you studied, and you walk into the exam with a slower, foggier brain. Material crammed in the middle of the night also tends to fade quickly, which hurts again on the cumulative final. If you find yourself facing an all-nighter, it is a sign to start earlier next time, not to push harder tonight. Chapter 12 covers sleep and stress in more depth.

What to eat and what to bring

You do not need a special "brain food" diet before an exam. You need to not be hungry, thirsty or jittery.

  • Eat a normal meal you are used to. Exam morning is not the day to try something new. A breakfast with some protein and fiber, like eggs and toast, yogurt and fruit, or oatmeal with peanut butter, tends to keep you going longer than a sugary snack alone.
  • Drink water. Bring a bottle if your exam setting allows it.
  • Keep caffeine normal. If you usually have one coffee, have one coffee. Doubling it or adding an energy drink can make you jittery, raise your heart rate, and add to anxious feelings. You will learn in Chapter 15 of OpenStax that caffeine and stress both push your body toward a sympathetic, fight-or-flight state, and you do not need extra of that on exam day.
  • Bring what the exam requires. For an in-person exam that may mean your student ID and pencils. For an online exam in Blackboard, it means a charged device, a reliable connection, and a quiet space. Follow your instructor's instructions exactly.
  • Leave everything else out of reach. The syllabus is clear that using any outside source or device during a quiz or test is cheating. Put your phone away and close other tabs and apps before you start.

Chapter 9

Exam Day and Test Anxiety

Strategies for the questions and for the nerves

You have studied. Now you have to show it, under a clock, with your heart beating a little faster than usual. This chapter gives you two sets of tools for exam day: strategies for multiple-choice questions, and strategies for the nerves that come with them.

Before the first question

Week 5 is usually when Exam 1 arrives in BIO 243. For many students, it is the first college science exam they have ever taken. It is normal to feel unsure about what it will be like. The good news is that a lot of what happens on exam day is in your control, starting before you read question one.

  • Arrive early, or log in early. If the exam is on campus, give yourself extra time for traffic, parking and finding a seat. If it is online in Blackboard, sit down at your device several minutes before you plan to start, with everything charged and other apps and tabs closed.
  • Read the instructions. How many questions are there? How much time do you have? Can you move back and forth between questions, or does the exam show them one at a time? This changes your pacing plan, as you will see below.
  • Do the time math. Divide the minutes by the number of questions to get your average time per question. Then pick two or three checkpoints. For example, if you have 50 questions in 60 minutes, you want to be around question 25 at about the 30-minute mark, with a little time left at the end.
  • Take one slow breath before you start. More on why that helps later in the chapter.

And one rule that never bends: the syllabus says that using any outside source or device during a quiz or test is cheating. That includes notes, websites, classmates, and AI. Put your phone away before you begin, so it is not even a temptation. Your own brain, trained by your practice, is the only tool you need.

Multiple-choice strategy

BIO 243 exams are multiple choice. That format rewards knowledge, of course, but it also rewards careful reading. Many points are lost not because a student did not know the content, but because they answered a slightly different question than the one on the page. Here is a five-step routine to use on every question until it becomes automatic.

  1. Read the whole stem. The stem is the question part, before the answer choices. Read every word, all the way to the end.
  2. Spot the key words. Words like NOT, EXCEPT, LEAST, FIRST, MOST LIKELY and BEST flip or narrow what the question wants. So do the specific terms: "osteoclast" is not "osteoblast," and "afferent" is not "efferent."
  3. Predict the answer before you look. Cover the choices with your hand or scroll so you cannot see them, and answer the question in your head first. Then look for your answer among the choices.
  4. Eliminate. Cross out choices that are clearly wrong. Every choice you remove raises your odds.
  5. Choose the best answer, then move on.

Why predicting works

Predicting your answer before you look is a small habit with a big effect. Wrong answer choices on a good exam are written to look tempting. If you read them first, they can pull you away from what you actually know. If you already have an answer in your head, you are checking the choices against it, instead of letting the choices lead you.

Take this question: "Which cell breaks down bone matrix during remodeling?" If you say "osteoclast" before you look, then you can quickly pass over "osteoblast," "osteocyte" and "chondrocyte," even though all three look and sound similar. If you look first, all four words blur together. Predicting is also a bonus round of retrieval practice, the strategy from Chapter 6.

True, but not the answer

The trickiest wrong answers are ones that are true statements, but do not answer the question asked. Look at this one:

What directly causes tropomyosin to move off the myosin-binding sites on actin? (a) Acetylcholine binding to receptors on the motor end plate, (b) calcium binding to troponin, (c) ATP binding to the myosin head, (d) sodium entering the muscle fiber.

Every one of these really happens during a muscle contraction. Acetylcholine does bind the motor end plate. Sodium does enter the fiber. ATP does bind myosin. But the word "directly" asks for the one step that moves tropomyosin, and that is (b), calcium binding to troponin. If you learned the process as a story with "because" links, as Chapter 7 suggested, you can place each choice in the story and see which step comes right before tropomyosin moves.

Watch the words that flip the question

Questions with NOT, EXCEPT or LEAST are easy to misread, especially when you are nervous. Your brain expects to look for a true statement, and it grabs the first true one it sees. Some habits that help:

  • Circle or underline the flip word if you are on paper. Online, say it in your head: "I am looking for the false one."
  • Treat each choice as its own true-or-false statement. Write T or F next to each one, mentally or on scratch paper if your exam allows it. The odd one out is the answer.
  • Be careful with absolute words like "always," "never" and "only" inside the choices. Biology has many exceptions, so a choice with an absolute word is often too strong. "Often" is not "always," though, so check it against what you know rather than ruling it out automatically.

A question reads: "Which of the following is NOT a type of connective tissue?" The choices are blood, bone, stratified squamous tissue, and adipose tissue. What is the best answer?

  1. Blood
  2. Bone
  3. Stratified squamous tissue
  4. Adipose tissue

Blood, bone and adipose tissue are all connective tissues. Stratified squamous is a type of epithelium, as in the epidermis. The word NOT means you are looking for the one false statement, so treating each choice as true or false finds it quickly.

Should you change your answer?

Many students have been told to "always go with your first instinct." It sounds wise, but the research does not support it as a rule. Studies of answer changing, going back many decades, have found that when students change an answer, the change is more often from wrong to right than from right to wrong.

Justin Kruger, Derrick Wirtz and Dale Miller (2005) looked at the answer changes of college students on a real exam and found the same pattern: more changes helped than hurt. They also found something interesting about why the myth survives. When a change turns out wrong, it stings and we remember it. When we stick with a first answer that was wrong, it bothers us less. So the painful memories pile up on the side of "never change." A study of medical students by Daniel Bauer and colleagues (2007) also found that answer changes were more often from wrong to right.

That does not mean you should change answers randomly. The research describes changes students chose to make, often for a reason. Here is a sensible rule:

  • Change your answer when you have a reason. You realized you misread the stem. You remembered a fact. A later question reminded you of something. You worked the problem again and got a different result.
  • Do not change it just because of a vague feeling that your first answer "seems too easy" or because you picked C three times in a row. Patterns in the answer key are not a reason.

Myth or fact?

"Your first instinct is usually right, so never change an answer."

Myth, at least as a hard rule. The research on answer changing generally shows that changes go from wrong to right more often than from right to wrong. The real rule is simpler: change an answer when you have a clear reason, and leave it alone when you only have a nervous feeling. If you reread a question about the epidermis and notice it asked for the deepest layer, not the outermost, changing from stratum corneum to stratum basale is exactly the right move.

Pacing: skip, mark and return

A single hard question can eat five minutes and shake your confidence. Meanwhile, three easy questions later in the exam are waiting for you. Good pacing protects you from that trap. The simplest method is the two-pass approach, shown in Figure 9.2.

A few more pacing tips:

  • Check your checkpoints. At each checkpoint you set at the start, glance at the clock. If you are behind, speed up on the questions you know and lean on the mark-and-return method for the rest.
  • Do not leave blanks unless the instructions say wrong answers cost extra points. A guess you narrowed down to two choices has a real chance of being right.
  • Use later questions as clues. Sometimes question 30 contains a term or idea that helps you with question 12. That is a good reason to revisit a marked question.
  • Know your exam's rules. Some online exams show one question at a time and do not let you go back. Your instructor's instructions tell you which kind you have. If you cannot go back, the "mark it" step becomes "make your best choice now," and the rest of the method still works.

You have 60 minutes for 50 questions. At the 30-minute mark you are on question 18 because you spent a long time on two hard questions. What is the best move?

  1. Keep going at the same pace and hope for easier questions
  2. Speed up: answer what you know quickly, mark the hard ones, and come back to them at the end
  3. Go back and recheck questions 1 to 18 before going on
  4. Skip all remaining questions about processes

You are behind your checkpoint of about question 25, so you need to bank the quick points first. The two-pass approach does that, while still giving the hard questions your leftover time.

Test anxiety is normal, and it is physiology

Some nervousness before an exam is almost universal. Your heart beats faster. Your breathing speeds up. Your palms get sweaty, your mouth gets dry, and your stomach feels fluttery. For some students those feelings are mild and pass quickly. For others they are strong enough to get in the way. A large review by Nathaniel von der Embse and colleagues (2018), which combined decades of studies, found that higher test anxiety is linked to somewhat lower test scores on average. The link is real but modest, and it does not mean anxious students are doomed. It means anxiety is worth managing.

Here is something useful about being an A&P student: you are going to learn exactly what is happening in your body. Those feelings are your sympathetic nervous system at work, the fight-or-flight division of the autonomic nervous system that you will study in Chapter 15 of OpenStax.

A&P in action

When your brain senses a challenge, the sympathetic division sends signals out through the body, and the adrenal medulla releases epinephrine (adrenaline) into the blood. Heart rate and the force of each beat go up, delivering more blood to your muscles and brain. The airways widen so more air moves in and out. Pupils dilate. Blood flow shifts away from the digestive tract, which is part of the "butterflies" feeling, and saliva production drops, which is why your mouth goes dry. Sweat glands switch on. Every one of these changes is your body getting ready to act. The opposite division, the parasympathetic, often called "rest and digest," slows the heart, in part through the vagus nerve (cranial nerve X). Slow breathing, especially with a long, slow exhale, is thought to lean the balance back toward that calmer state.

Knowing the physiology helps in a practical way. A pounding heart before Exam 1 is not a sign that something is wrong with you, or a sign that you will fail. It is a normal, ancient system doing its job. The question is how you respond to it. The tools below help.

Tools for the nerves

1. Slow breathing

Breathing is the one part of the stress response you can control directly. A simple pattern: breathe in through your nose for a count of four, then breathe out slowly through your mouth for a count of six. Repeat for one to two minutes. A systematic review by Andrea Zaccaro and colleagues (2018) found that slow breathing techniques, at around six breaths per minute, were linked with signs of increased parasympathetic activity and with reduced feelings of anxiety and stress in a number of studies. The number of high-quality studies is still fairly small, so think of slow breathing as a helpful tool rather than a cure. It costs nothing, it takes a minute, and nobody can tell you are doing it.

Try it now

Set a timer for one minute. Sit up, feet flat, shoulders down. Breathe in through your nose for four counts. Breathe out slowly through slightly pursed lips for six counts, as if you were blowing on hot soup. Keep going until the timer ends. Notice anything in your shoulders, jaw or heart rate? Practice this a few times this week, while you are calm, so it is a familiar skill on exam day. Then use it in the minutes before the exam starts, and again if you hit a wall in the middle.

2. Write your worries down

Worry takes up space in working memory, the small mental workspace you use to hold and juggle information while you think (Chapter 5 covers it). If part of that workspace is busy with "what if I fail," less is left for the question about joint types. Gerardo Ramirez and Sian Beilock (2011) tested a simple idea: right before an exam, students spent about ten minutes writing openly about their thoughts and feelings about the test. In their studies, which included a high school biology class, students with high test anxiety who did this writing performed better than similarly anxious students who did not. Later research has found smaller or mixed effects, so it is not a guarantee. But it is easy to try. Before your next exam, or the night before, spend five to ten minutes writing honestly about what is worrying you. Then put the paper away.

3. Reframe the feeling: "This is my body getting ready"

The same racing heart can be read two ways. You can tell yourself a threat story: "I am panicking, this is bad." Or you can tell yourself a challenge story: "My body is getting ready to perform." This is called reappraisal. In a study by Jeremy Jamieson and colleagues (2010), students preparing for the GRE, a graduate school admissions test, were told before a practice test that feeling anxious and aroused does not hurt performance and can even help it. Those students scored higher on the math section than students who did not get that message, and their reported scores on the real GRE were also higher. It was one fairly small study, so do not expect magic. But the message is true to physiology: the sympathetic response really is a get-ready response.

4. Preparation is the best calming tool

The most reliable way to feel calmer on exam day is to have practiced under exam-like conditions. If you took a timed, closed-book practice test a few days earlier, as Chapter 8 suggested, the real exam feels familiar. You have already seen yourself answer hard questions under a clock. Familiar is less frightening.

5. If you blank in the middle

Almost everyone has had the moment where you read a question and your mind goes white. When it happens:

  • Stop. Take two slow breaths with long exhales.
  • Mark the question and move to the next one. Getting a few answers right rebuilds momentum.
  • When you come back, try to find a way in. What chapter is this from? What figure would it be in? What is the story the question is part of? Often one detail brings the rest back.
  • If you still do not know, narrow it down and make your best guess. One question is one question.

Picture this

Imagine a student named Lena, a fictional first-generation student who wants to be a respiratory therapist. Before Exam 1, her heart pounded so hard that she read the first question three times without taking it in. For Exam 2, she tried a new routine. The night before, she wrote for ten minutes about what was scaring her, mostly the fear of disappointing her family, and then put the page in a drawer. In the parking lot, she did one minute of four-in, six-out breathing. When her heart sped up at question one, she said to herself, "Sympathetic response. My heart is pumping more blood to my brain. I'm ready." She still felt nervous. But this time the nerves felt like energy instead of a wall, and she could read each stem all the way to the end.

Chapter 10

After the Exam: Read Your Results

Turn a score into a plan for the next one

When an exam comes back, most students look at the number, feel something about it, and move on. The students who improve the fastest do one more thing: they study the exam itself. This chapter shows you how to read your results, sort your mistakes, and turn them into a short, specific plan for the next exam, including what to do when the number is low.

The score is information, not a verdict

Let's be honest about the first hour. If the score is good, you feel relief. If it is lower than you hoped, it can sting, and you might feel embarrassed, angry, or worried about your plans for nursing or another health program. Those feelings are normal. Give yourself a little time with them. Go for a walk, talk to someone you trust, and get some sleep.

Then, within a few days, come back to the exam with a different question. Not "Am I good at this?" but "What does this tell me?" An exam score is information about how a particular set of study methods worked on a particular set of questions. It is not a measure of how smart you are or whether you belong in science. Chapter 1 talked about how common it is for capable students to struggle at first in A&P. The students who end up doing well are often the ones who use that first struggle as data.

Researchers who study self-regulated learning, such as Barry Zimmerman (2002), describe strong learners as working in a cycle. They plan how to study, they do the studying while watching how it is going, and then they reflect on the results and adjust. Most students do the first two parts and skip the third. Figure 10.1 shows the cycle as it looks in BIO 243, and where this book covers each part.

This chapter lines up with week 6, just after Exam 1 usually comes back, and week 9, just after Exam 2. Both are perfect moments to close the loop. Week 9 also tends to fall close to the point in the term when some students start weighing bigger decisions, which the last part of this chapter covers.

The exam wrapper

An exam wrapper is a short reflection sheet you fill out after an exam is returned. The idea comes from Marsha Lovett (2013), who studies learning at Carnegie Mellon University. Instead of just looking at the score, the wrapper asks you three kinds of questions: how you prepared, what kinds of mistakes you made, and what you will do differently next time. The name comes from the way it "wraps" around the exam. Lovett's point is that the exam already holds a lot of information about your learning, and the wrapper helps you actually use it. The book How Learning Works (Ambrose and colleagues, 2010), which Lovett co-wrote, describes this kind of reflection as a core part of becoming a self-directed learner.

First, find out how you can review the exam

Instructors handle exam review in different ways. Some let you see your answers in Blackboard. Some go over common mistakes in class. Some ask you to review the exam during student hours. Check your syllabus or ask your instructor how review works in your section. Whatever the method, keep it honest: do not copy, photograph or share exam questions unless your instructor has said that is allowed. The goal is to understand your mistakes, not to collect the questions.

Your exam wrapper questions

Answer these in writing, in a notebook or a note on your phone. It takes about fifteen minutes.

  1. How did I prepare? Roughly how many hours, over how many days? What did I actually do: reread, highlight, flashcards, blank diagrams, practice questions, a practice test?
  2. What did I expect my score to be, and how close was I? A big gap between what you expected and what you got is useful information on its own. It often means your study methods made you feel ready without testing whether you were.
  3. Where did I lose points? List each miss by topic, then sort it into one of the four bins in the next section.
  4. Which bin had the most misses? That is your biggest lever.
  5. What two or three specific things will I change for the next exam?
  6. Who will I ask for help, and when?

Sorting your misses

Not all wrong answers are the same kind of wrong. A question you missed because you had never really learned the topic needs a very different fix from a question you missed because you skipped over the word NOT. Sorting your misses into bins shows you which fix you need most. Figure 10.2 shows four common bins.

Bin 1: Did not know it

You had not learned this material, or you had learned it too weakly to recall. Maybe you never got to that section, or you read it once and moved on. For example, if you missed a question about the tissue that lines the urinary bladder and had no idea it was transitional epithelium, which can stretch as the bladder fills, that is a Bin 1 miss. The fix is more and better study of that content: the strategies in Chapters 6 and 7, and, if a lot of misses land here, more total study time, starting earlier, as Chapter 8 describes.

Bin 2: Knew it, but misread or slipped

You knew the content, but you misread the stem, missed a flip word, mixed up two similar terms in the moment, or clicked the wrong choice. These hurt the most, because the knowledge was there. The fix is the exam-day routine from Chapter 9: read the whole stem, spot the key words, predict before you look, and treat each choice as true or false on NOT and EXCEPT questions.

Bin 3: Ran out of time

You rushed, guessed, or left questions at the end because time ran short. The fix is pacing: set checkpoints at the start, use the two-pass approach, and practice under timed conditions so the real clock feels familiar.

Bin 4: Studied the wrong thing, or the wrong way

This bin is sneaky, and it is often the biggest one on the first A&P exam. You studied, and you even recognized the terms on the exam, but you could not use them. Maybe you memorized the names of the organelles but could not say which one would be most active in a cell that makes lots of protein for export (rough endoplasmic reticulum, along with the Golgi apparatus). Maybe you made flashcards of terms but the exam asked about processes. This usually means your studying was built on recognition, like rereading and looking at cards, rather than recall and application. The fix is to study in the form the exam uses: practice questions, "what if" questions, and explaining processes out loud from memory. The review by John Dunlosky and colleagues (2013) is a good reminder of why: rereading and highlighting were rated as low-utility techniques, and practice testing as high.

Try it now

Here are five imaginary misses from an Exam 1. Sort each into Bin 1, 2, 3 or 4.

  1. "I knew osmosis, but I didn't notice the question said hypertonic, not hypotonic."
  2. "I had never studied the phases of mitosis. I ran out of days."
  3. "I could define negative feedback but couldn't tell which example was one."
  4. "Questions 44 to 50 were guesses. I was out of time."
  5. "I read about the four tissue types twice, but I couldn't identify one from a description."

Answers: 1 is Bin 2 (misread), 2 is Bin 1 (did not know), 3 is Bin 4 (could define but not apply), 4 is Bin 3 (time), 5 is Bin 4 (rereading built recognition, not recall). If two or more of your own misses land in Bin 4, the switch to retrieval practice in Chapter 6 is probably your single biggest change.

On Exam 1, a student recognized almost every term but missed many questions that described a situation and asked what would happen. Which bin, and which fix, fits best?

  1. Bin 3: work on pacing
  2. Bin 2: read the stem more slowly
  3. Bin 1: study more hours of the same kind
  4. Bin 4: switch from recognizing terms to practicing questions and explaining processes from memory

Recognizing terms but failing to apply them is the sign of studying the wrong way. The fix is to practice in the form the exam uses: answering application questions and explaining processes without notes.

Make a change plan

The most common post-exam plan is "study harder." It almost never works, because it does not say what to do differently. A good change plan has two or three specific changes, each one tied to a bin, and each one written so you could check whether you did it.

A helpful way to write each change is as an if-then plan, which psychologists call an implementation intention. Instead of a goal like "do more practice questions," you write the situation and the action: "If it is Tuesday or Thursday after my shift, then I do 15 practice questions before I open the textbook." Peter Gollwitzer (1999) reviewed research showing that if-then plans like this make people more likely to follow through on goals than simply setting the goal. They work because the situation becomes a cue that triggers the action, so you do not have to decide again each time.

Some examples of vague plans turned into specific ones:

  • Vague: "Study more." Specific: "Start exam prep ten days out, and use the Exam Planner tool on the first day."
  • Vague: "Be more careful." Specific: "On every exam question, I predict the answer before looking at the choices, and I say the flip word in my head."
  • Vague: "Understand the material better." Specific: "If I finish reading a section, then I close the book and do a two-minute blank-page recall before moving on."
  • Vague: "Get help." Specific: "This Wednesday I go to the SI session, and this week I go to student hours with my two toughest questions written down."

A&P in action

Here is a change plan a student might write in week 6, after Exam 1, heading into the Exam 2 unit. Their wrapper showed most misses in Bin 4, especially tissue identification, and a few in Bin 2.

Change 1 (Bin 4): "If I finish an OpenStax section with a labeled figure, then I make a blank version and climb the ladder from Chapter 7. First up: the layers of the epidermis, from deepest to most superficial: stratum basale, spinosum, granulosum, lucidum (in thick skin only) and corneum."

Notice the plan uses Chapter 5 and Chapter 6 content right away. A change plan works best when it starts on the very next study session, not on the week before the next exam.

Bring your results to student hours

Student hours, also called office hours, are time your instructor sets aside to help students. Many students avoid them, especially after a low score, because they feel embarrassed. But instructors generally want to see students after a hard exam. It shows you are taking the course seriously, and it gives them a chance to help before the gap gets bigger.

The key is to bring a specific question. "I don't get it" is hard to answer in ten minutes. "Can you help me see the difference between facilitated diffusion and active transport? I keep mixing them up on questions about which one uses ATP" is easy to answer, and you will leave with something useful. Bring your exam wrapper. It shows exactly what you need.

Good questions to bring after an exam:

  • "Most of my misses were application questions. Could you show me how you would think through one like that?"
  • "I studied about six hours using flashcards and rereading. Based on my results, what would you change?"
  • "Is there a topic from this unit that the next unit builds on, so I should fix it first?"

Your instructor is not the only help. Supplemental Instruction (SI) sessions, led by students who did well in the course, run weekly on the hardest content. Peer Tutoring through QuadC and the Student Success Center are there too. If you receive a Starfish message from your instructor or advisor after an exam, read it as an invitation, not a punishment. It means someone noticed and wants to help. Chapter 13, Help Is a Strategy, has email scripts and more on each of these.

Chapter 11

Using AI the Right Way in BIO 243

A patient tutor, if you use it like one

AI chatbots can quiz you at midnight, explain the sliding filament model five different ways, and never get tired of your questions. They can also tell you something wrong about the cranial nerves in a calm, confident voice. This chapter shows you how to get the first and protect yourself from the second.

What generative AI is, and what it is not

Tools like ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude and Copilot are called generative AI. You type a message, called a prompt, and the tool writes a reply. The replies are often clear, friendly and well organized. That makes it easy to forget what is actually happening behind the screen.

Here is the plain version. These tools are built on large language models, trained on a huge amount of text. From that text they learned patterns: which words tend to follow which, in which kinds of sentences. When you ask a question, the model builds an answer one small piece at a time, each time choosing a piece that is likely to come next. It is a very powerful pattern machine.

Notice what is missing. The model does not look up the answer in OpenStax unless you give it the text. It does not "know" the resting membrane potential the way you will after you study. For common A&P topics, the likely answer is usually the correct one. Sometimes it is not, and the wrong answer sounds exactly as sure as the right one.

Computer scientists call these confident mistakes hallucinations. The word is a little dramatic. It does not mean the tool is broken. It means the tool did what it always does, produce likely text, and the likely text happened to be false. Researchers who review this problem describe it as a built-in risk of how these models generate language, not a rare glitch (Ji et al., 2023).

Good at, bad at

For studying, AI is good at explaining an idea another way (when the OpenStax paragraph on excitation-contraction coupling did not click), making practice questions, quizzing you patiently at any hour, checking an explanation you wrote, and organizing a messy week into a plan.

It is bad at exact facts and numbers, where small errors hide; at your course specifics, like what your instructor emphasized or which chapters are on Exam 2; and at sources, which it may invent. Most of all, it cannot do your learning for you. It can write a perfect summary of chapter 10. Reading that summary will not put chapter 10 into your memory.

Myth or fact?

"If the AI sounds sure, it is probably right." Myth. A language model does not have a reliable inner sense of when it is guessing. It can explain a wrong idea in smooth, well-organized sentences with no hint of doubt. Confidence in the writing tells you about the writing, not about the facts. Your check is OpenStax.

The five BIO 243 AI rules

These five rules apply to everything in this Success Lab. They are short on purpose, so you can remember them when it counts.

  1. Use AI as a tutor, not a ghostwriter. Ask it to quiz you, explain a topic another way, or check your explanation. It is fine for studying.
  2. No AI on quizzes, exams or graded work (Guided Practice, Reading Guide, CircleIn posts) unless the assignment says so. Never paste quiz or exam questions into AI.
  3. Check AI against the textbook. AI makes confident mistakes. OpenStax and your notes win.
  4. Keep it private. Never paste in personal information: yours or anyone else's (student ID, grades, health details, classmates' names).
  5. Every class is different. USC Upstate leaves many AI decisions to each instructor. In other courses, follow that syllabus, and when in doubt, ask before you use it. If AI use is allowed on an assignment, say how you used it.

Rule 2 connects straight to the BIO 243 syllabus. The syllabus treats presenting AI work as your own as an academic integrity violation, and it treats using any outside source or device during a quiz or test as cheating. Studying together is wonderful. Graded work is answered alone. AI counts as "together" when you study and as "an outside source" when you are being graded.

Why "tutor" beats "ghostwriter"

There is a learning reason behind rule 1, not just a rule-following reason. In Chapter 6 you met retrieval practice: pulling information out of your memory is one of the strongest ways to make it stick (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006). The effort is the point. When AI hands you a finished answer, you skip the effort, and you skip most of the learning with it.

A large study with high school math students in Turkey tested this directly (Bastani et al., 2025). Some students practiced with a plain chatbot that would simply give answers. Others practiced with a version set up as a tutor that gave hints instead of answers. The plain chatbot helped students do better on the practice problems while they had it. But on a later test without AI, those students did worse than students who never had the chatbot at all. The tutor-style version largely avoided that harm. The study was in math, not A&P, so treat it as a warning sign rather than the final word: the tool made practice easier, and easier practice built less skill.

So the goal is not "use AI less." The goal is to make AI ask you the questions.

Picture this

Imagine two fictional BIO 243 students, Dev and Rosa, both studying chapter 10 for Exam 2. Dev asks a chatbot to "summarize muscle contraction." He reads the summary three times. It feels clear and familiar. Rosa asks the chatbot to "quiz me on muscle contraction, one question at a time, and wait for my answer." She gets three of the first eight wrong, checks each one in OpenStax section 10.3, and makes a flashcard for every miss. Rosa's evening felt harder. On exam day, she is the one who can explain why ATP is needed for a myosin head to let go of actin. Dev recognizes the words but cannot pull them up on his own.

Which use of AI fits rule 1, "tutor, not ghostwriter"?

  1. Asking AI to fill in your OpenStax Reading Guide so you can study from it
  2. Asking AI to quiz you on epithelial tissue and wait for your answers
  3. Asking AI to write your CircleIn post about bone remodeling
  4. Pasting a Blackboard quiz question to see how AI would answer it

A tutor makes you do the thinking. Being quizzed is retrieval practice. The Reading Guide and CircleIn posts are graded work, and quiz questions never go into AI.

How to write a good study prompt

A vague prompt ("explain muscles") gets a vague essay. A strong study prompt usually has five parts:

  1. Who you are. "I am a pre-nursing student in Anatomy and Physiology I." This sets the level.
  2. The exact topic, tied to your book. "The action potential, OpenStax Anatomy and Physiology chapter 12." This narrows the answer.
  3. The job. Quiz me, explain another way, or check my explanation. Pick one.
  4. The format. "One question at a time. Wait for my answer." Or "a numbered list of steps." Or "answers at the bottom."
  5. A safety line. "If you are not sure about something, say so. Do not make up sources." This does not make the tool perfect, but it helps.

Then add the most important step, which happens after the answer: check it. Every worked example below ends with exactly what to look up.

Smart AI move

Add one line to the end of any quiz prompt: "After each answer I give, ask me to explain why, before you tell me if I am right." This turns a simple recall quiz into self-explanation, which pushes you past recognizing a term to understanding it. It also slows you down in a good way.

Each example shows a prompt you could type, what a useful answer looks like, and exactly what to check in OpenStax. Section numbers refer to the free OpenStax Anatomy and Physiology textbook. If your edition numbers sections a little differently, use the section title.

Example 1: Quiz me on tissue types (chapter 4)

Prompt: "I am a pre-nursing student in Anatomy and Physiology I, using the OpenStax textbook, chapter 4. Quiz me on the four basic tissue types. Ask one question at a time and wait for my answer. After I answer, tell me if I was right and why."

A useful answer asks a single question and then stops, for example: "Which tissue type covers body surfaces and lines body cavities?" It waits. When you answer, it gives a short reason and moves on. Good questions mix levels: some ask for a name, some ask you to match a function to a tissue, some give a short scenario.

Check in OpenStax, section 4.1 (Types of Tissues): the four types are epithelial, connective, muscle and nervous. Check the functions the AI gives. Epithelial tissue covers and lines. Connective tissue binds, supports and protects, and it includes bone, cartilage, fat and blood. Muscle tissue contracts. Nervous tissue sends electrical and chemical signals. One mistake to watch for: some answers treat blood as if it were not a tissue at all. OpenStax classifies blood as a fluid connective tissue.

Example 2: Explain the sliding filament model another way (chapter 10)

Prompt: "Explain the sliding filament model of skeletal muscle contraction as if I were a first-year nursing student. Use a short numbered list of steps and one everyday comparison. Then ask me two questions to check my understanding."

A useful answer walks through the cross-bridge cycle in order. Calcium is released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Calcium binds to troponin, which moves tropomyosin off the binding sites on actin. Myosin heads attach to actin and pull the thin filaments toward the center of the sarcomere (the power stroke). A new ATP binds to the myosin head, which makes the head let go. Splitting that ATP re-cocks the head so it can grab again. The cycle repeats as long as calcium and ATP are available. Then it asks you questions instead of stopping.

Check in OpenStax, section 10.3 (Muscle Fiber Contraction and Relaxation) and the sarcomere figures in 10.2. Two errors to hunt for. First, some answers say ATP is needed for myosin to attach to actin. In OpenStax, ATP binding is what lets myosin detach. That is why muscles lock up in rigor mortis when ATP runs out. Second, watch what the answer says about the bands. The filaments do not shorten; they slide. Figure 11.3 shows what changes.

Example 3: Check my explanation of an action potential (chapter 12)

Prompt: "Here is my explanation of a neuron's action potential, written from memory. Do not rewrite it for me. Point out anything wrong or missing, and ask me a question that helps me fix it myself. My explanation: At rest the inside of the neuron is about -70 mV. A stimulus brings it to threshold, and sodium channels open so sodium rushes in. Then the sodium-potassium pump pushes the sodium back out, which repolarizes the cell."

This is the best kind of prompt: you did the work first, from memory, and the AI acts like a study partner reading your draft.

A useful answer praises what is right (resting potential, threshold, sodium entering) and flags the problem in the last sentence. Repolarization happens mainly because voltage-gated sodium channels inactivate and voltage-gated potassium channels open, so potassium flows out of the cell. The sodium-potassium pump keeps the ion gradients in place over time, but it is not what brings the membrane back down during a single action potential. A good tutor then asks you something like: "Which ion leaves the cell during repolarization, and through what kind of channel?"

Check in OpenStax, section 12.4 (The Action Potential): resting potential about -70 mV, threshold about -55 mV, a peak around +30 mV, then repolarization and a brief hyperpolarization. Also check the phrase "all or none." If the answer says an action potential can be "partly" fired or gets bigger with a bigger stimulus, that is wrong for an action potential.

An AI explanation says, "During repolarization, the sodium-potassium pump moves sodium out of the neuron." What should you do?

  1. Accept it, since it explained the rest of the process clearly
  2. Ask the AI if it is sure, and accept its second answer
  3. Paste it into your Guided Practice so you have it saved
  4. Check OpenStax 12.4: repolarization is driven mainly by potassium leaving through voltage-gated potassium channels

Rule 3: OpenStax and your notes win. Asking the AI again is not a real check, because it can be confidently wrong twice. And Guided Practice is graded work.

Example 4: Compare the phases of mitosis (chapter 3)

Prompt: "Make a simple table comparing the phases of mitosis: what the chromosomes, nuclear envelope and spindle are doing in each phase. Use the phase names from the OpenStax Anatomy and Physiology textbook, chapter 3."

A useful answer gives a clean table you can then cover up and fill in from memory, which turns it into self-testing. It should keep interphase separate, because interphase is not part of mitosis; it is the long stretch when the cell grows and copies its DNA.

Check in OpenStax, section 3.5 (Cell Growth and Division): OpenStax lists prophase, prometaphase, metaphase, anaphase and telophase, with cytokinesis as the splitting of the cytoplasm. Many other sources teach four phases and leave out prometaphase. An AI trained on all of them may give you the four-phase version even when you asked for OpenStax. Neither is "wrong" science, but your exam follows your course. When the AI and your textbook use different names or counts, use the textbook's.

Example 5: Practice the cranial nerves (chapters 13 and 16)

Prompt: "Give me 10 mixed practice questions on the 12 cranial nerves. Cover the name, the Roman numeral, whether it is sensory, motor or both, and one way it is tested in a neurological exam. Put the questions first and the answers at the very bottom."

A useful answer mixes question types instead of going I through XII in order, and answers at the bottom let you try first.

Check in OpenStax, section 13.4 (The Peripheral Nervous System) for the nerve list and section 16.3 (The Cranial Nerve Exam) for the tests. The eye-movement nerves are a common place for mix-ups. The oculomotor nerve (III) moves most of the eye muscles. The trochlear nerve (IV) controls the superior oblique. The abducens nerve (VI) controls the lateral rectus. An AI answer that swaps IV and VI reads perfectly smoothly, which is exactly why you check. Many students remember it as "LR6, SO4": lateral rectus by six, superior oblique by four.

A&P in action

The errors in Examples 4 and 5 are not "dumb." Both are plausible: a real alternate way of teaching mitosis, and two eye nerves that sit side by side in every list. That is what AI mistakes in A&P usually look like: almost right, in a place where almost right costs you a question.

Example 6: Build a mixed review for Exam 2 (chapters 5, 6, 9 and 10)

Prompt: "Make a 12-question multiple-choice practice set that jumps between skin layers, bone cells, joint types and muscle tissue, in a mixed order. Put the answers at the end, each with a one-sentence reason. These are for my own practice, not for a graded assignment."

A useful answer really mixes the topics. Mixing forces you to decide which idea a question is about, which is the interleaving you met in Chapter 6. This is AI-made practice, not your real quiz. You are never pasting in Blackboard quiz questions.

Check in OpenStax. In section 5.1 (Layers of the Skin), the epidermis layers from deep to surface are stratum basale, spinosum, granulosum, lucidum and corneum, and the stratum lucidum is found only in thick skin, such as the palms and soles. In section 6.3 (Bone Structure), osteoblasts build bone matrix, osteocytes maintain it, and osteoclasts break it down. In chapter 9, joints are classified by structure (fibrous, cartilaginous, synovial) and by function (synarthrosis, amphiarthrosis, diarthrosis). Swapped pairs, like osteoblast and osteoclast, are a favorite AI slip. If even one answer key item is wrong, fix it in your copy, then trust the rest of the set a little less.

Example 7: Turn my own notes into flashcards (chapter 1)

Prompt: "Below are my own notes on homeostasis and feedback loops. Turn them into 8 flashcards with a question on the front and a short answer on the back. Do not add facts that are not in my notes. If you think something important is missing, list it separately at the end. [paste your notes]"

A useful answer stays inside your notes, so the cards match what your class covered. The separate "might be missing" list is a prompt for you to go look, not a list of facts to accept.

Check in OpenStax, section 1.5 (Homeostasis): negative feedback pushes a variable back toward its set point, like body temperature regulation. Positive feedback pushes a change further in the same direction until an endpoint, like childbirth contractions intensified by oxytocin, or blood clotting. If a card calls blood glucose control "positive feedback," that card is wrong. Also notice what you pasted: your own study notes. Not a graded assignment, and nothing personal.

What AI mistakes look like in A&P

Check enough answers and you will see the same kinds of errors again and again:

  • Swapped pairs. Osteoblast and osteoclast. Afferent (toward the CNS) and efferent (away from it). Sympathetic and parasympathetic effects in chapter 15. Trochlear and abducens.
  • Plausible but wrong terms. A real-sounding word in the wrong place, like calling a band of the sarcomere by the wrong letter.
  • Wrong numbers. A threshold, a count of bones, a number of layers. Close numbers are the hardest to spot.
  • Oversimplified until wrong. "The pump repolarizes the neuron" is a simplification that crosses into error.
  • Different from your course. Correct in some textbook, but not how OpenStax or your instructor teaches it, like the four-phase mitosis list.
  • Invented sources. Ask for references and you may get a journal article with real-sounding authors, a real journal name, and a title that does not exist. When researchers checked citations that ChatGPT wrote for literature reviews, many were made up, and the newer model, while better, still invented some (Walters & Wilder, 2023). Never put an AI-supplied source into any paper without finding the actual source yourself.
  • Labels on images. If you ask an AI to label a diagram, or describe a picture of a bone, treat every label as a guess until you match it to an OpenStax figure.

Try it now

Open any chatbot and ask: "In 3 sentences, what are the four basic tissue types and one function of each? Then give me one source for this." Now open OpenStax section 4.1 and check every claim. Then search for the source it gave you. Did the source exist, exactly as named? Two minutes, and you will have run the whole check loop once.

The lines you do not cross

Some uses of AI are not weak studying. They are integrity violations under the BIO 243 syllabus. Be clear on these:

  • Quizzes and exams. No AI, no outside sources, no second device. This includes Blackboard quizzes that are open for several days. "Open for a while" does not mean "open book."
  • Pasting quiz or exam questions into AI, including screenshots, retyped versions, or "just to check my answer afterward." Quiz content stays in Blackboard.
  • Guided Practice and the Reading Guide. These are graded. The Reading Guide is also one of your best study tools because you find the answers in the textbook yourself.
  • CircleIn posts. CircleIn is graded and built on students helping students. A post written by AI is not your contribution, even if you edit it a bit.

Some uses are allowed but weak. "Summarize chapter 13 so I do not have to read it" breaks no rule, but leaves you with familiar words and little you can recall. Use summaries, if at all, after you read, to compare with your own notes.

Keep it private

When you type into a chatbot, your words go to a company's servers, where, depending on the tool and your settings, they may be stored, reviewed, or used to improve future models. No need to be fearful. Just be careful what you hand over.

Rule 4 says never paste in personal information, yours or anyone else's. That includes your student ID, your grades, health details, and classmates' names. It also includes things that seem harmless in the moment, like a screenshot of your Blackboard grade center or a group chat with names in it.

You can almost always get the same help without the personal details. Instead of "I got a 61 on my first exam and I have ADHD, make me a plan," try "I want to raise my exam scores in Anatomy and Physiology I. I work 20 hours a week and commute. Build me a weekly study plan with short sessions." You still get a useful plan. Your grade and your health stay with you. If you need support with a disability or a hard semester, those are conversations for Disability Services, Counseling Services, or your instructor, as Chapter 13 explains.

Which prompt follows rule 4, "keep it private"?

  1. "My student ID is ... can you help me read my unofficial transcript?"
  2. "My lab partner Jasmine keeps missing class. Write her a message."
  3. "Quiz me on the bones of the axial skeleton, one question at a time."
  4. "Here is a screenshot of my Blackboard grades. What do I need on the final?"

Only the quiz prompt contains no personal information. Student IDs, grades and classmates' names stay out of AI tools.

Every class is different: asking and disclosing

USC Upstate leaves many decisions about AI to each instructor, so a use that is fine in one course can be a violation in the next. BIO 243's rules are the ones in this chapter. For every other course, the syllabus for that course is your guide.

When the syllabus is unclear, ask before you use AI, not after. A short message is enough: "For the case study assignment, may I use an AI tool to quiz myself on the material before I write? I would write the assignment itself on my own." Instructors respect that question. It shows you take integrity seriously.

When an assignment does allow AI, say how you used it. A one or two sentence note works well: "I used a chatbot to generate practice questions on the cardiac cycle while studying. I wrote this report myself and checked all facts against the course textbook." That honesty protects you, and it is a habit you will need in nursing and health care, where people need to trust where your information came from.

Smart AI move

Keep a small "AI log" in your notes for the week: the date's topic, what you asked, and what you found when you checked OpenStax. Two lines per session. Over a few weeks you will see which topics the AI handles well and which it fumbles, and you will have a record ready if a course ever asks how you used AI.

Putting it together in a real study week

Here is balanced AI use in a normal week, around weeks 10 and 11 when chapters 12 and 13 pile up.

  1. Read first. Read the OpenStax section before class and work on the Reading Guide on your own.
  2. Write from memory. After lecture, write your own explanation of the action potential or the spinal cord tracts without looking.
  3. Use AI to check. Paste in your own explanation with the "do not rewrite, point out what is wrong" prompt from Example 3.
  4. Verify. Look up every correction in OpenStax before you accept it.
  5. Use AI to quiz. Later in the week, ask for a mixed practice set and answer it without notes.
  6. Make it yours. Every miss becomes a flashcard you write, in CircleIn or on paper.

Notice where AI sits: in the middle of your studying, never in place of reading and never in place of you. Used like this, AI is not a shortcut around the hard part of A&P. It is more practice at the hard part, with a study partner you double-check every time.

You are in a different course whose syllabus does not mention AI at all. You want to use a chatbot to brainstorm topics for a paper. What is the best move?

  1. Ask the instructor first, and if it is allowed, say how you used it
  2. Use it, because the syllabus did not forbid it
  3. Use it, but delete the chat so there is no record
  4. Follow the BIO 243 rules, since they apply to every course

Rule 5: every class is different. When in doubt, ask before you use it, and disclose your use when AI is allowed.

Chapter 12

Motivation, Stress, Sleep and Setbacks

Taking care of the person who does the studying

By week 12, BIO 243 has asked a lot of you: three chapters of nervous system, a third exam on the horizon, and a life that did not pause for the semester. This chapter is about the engine behind every study plan in this book, which is you.

Motivation usually follows action

Most people believe motivation works like this: first you feel like studying, then you study. So they wait to feel like it. Some nights the feeling never shows up, and the chapter on the autonomic nervous system stays closed.

For many tasks, it works the other way around. You start, even a little, and the feeling of "okay, I can do this" shows up after. Starting is the hardest part of most study sessions. Once you are three minutes into a page, the task looks smaller than it did from the couch. A small win, like getting two flashcards right, gives you a reason to keep going.

This is good news, because it means you do not need to fix your mood before you can study. You need a start small enough that your mood does not get a vote.

Make the start tiny

A tiny start is a task so small it feels almost silly to refuse:

  • "Open OpenStax to section 15.1 and read only the first heading and the first figure."
  • "Do five CircleIn flashcards."
  • "Write the parts of a reflex arc from memory, then stop if I want to."
  • "Set a timer for 10 minutes on the Reading Guide."

The rule is that you are allowed to stop when the tiny task is done. Most nights, you will not want to. Some nights you will stop, and that is fine. Ten minutes of retrieval practice is far better than zero minutes of guilt.

Picture this

Imagine a fictional student named Tasha. She works as a patient care tech and gets home from a long shift tired and sore. Her plan says "study chapter 15 for an hour," and every night that plan loses. So she changes it to "sit at the kitchen table and do five flashcards before I shower." Some nights she stops at five. Most nights she does twenty, then reads one section, because once she has started, stopping feels stranger than continuing. Tasha did not become more motivated. She made the start small enough to win.

Connect the task to your why

Tiny starts get you moving. A reason keeps you moving across a long semester. Many BIO 243 students are headed for nursing or another health career, but on a tired Thursday night, "memorize the spinal nerve plexuses" can feel unrelated to that goal. It is not. The brachial plexus is why a nurse checks hand strength and sensation after a shoulder injury. The neurological exam in chapter 16 is something you may one day help perform.

Researchers have tested a simple version of this idea. In one study with high school science students, some wrote short essays about how the course material connected to their own lives. Among students who had been expecting to do poorly, those essay writers became more interested in science and earned better grades (Hulleman & Harackiewicz, 2009). One study is not a guarantee, but writing a few sentences costs you almost nothing. Try it with whatever chapter you are on: "This matters to me because..."

Habits and cues: let your day do the remembering

A habit is a behavior that runs mostly on autopilot, set off by a cue in your day. Brushing your teeth is a habit. You do not decide each morning whether to do it. Something in your routine triggers it.

Studying can work the same way, at least partly. In one well-known study, people picked a new daily behavior, like a healthy snack or a short walk, and tied it to a cue in their day. On average it took a little over two months for the behavior to feel automatic, and the range between people was huge, from a few weeks to most of a year. Missing a single day did not ruin the process (Lally et al., 2010). So be patient with yourself, and do not give up after one bad day.

If-then plans

One of the most studied tools for following through is the if-then plan, which researchers call an implementation intention. Instead of "I will study more," you decide exactly when and where: "If it is Tuesday after lab, then I will go to the library and do the chapter 13 Reading Guide for 30 minutes." A review that combined results from many studies found that people who made plans like these reached their goals noticeably more often than people who only set the goal (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006). It is not magic, but it is a cheap, real advantage.

Good cues are things that already happen every day:

  • "When I sit down on the bus, I open my flashcards."
  • "When I park on campus, before I get out of the car, I review yesterday's missed cards."
  • "When my kids go to bed, I set a 25-minute timer for BIO 243."
  • "When I finish dinner on Sunday, I plan my study blocks for the week."

Try it now

Write two if-then plans for this week. Each one needs a real cue (something that already happens), a place, and a tiny, specific task. For example: "If I finish my Thursday shift, then I will do ten chapter 14 flashcards in the break room before I drive home." Put them where you will see them.

Self-compassion is not going easy on yourself

Listen to the voice in your head after a bad quiz. For many students it sounds something like: "You are so stupid. Everyone else gets this. You will never make it into nursing." Most of us would never talk to a friend that way.

Self-compassion means treating yourself the way you would treat a friend who is struggling: honestly, but kindly. It has three parts. You notice that you are hurting instead of pretending you are fine. You remember that struggling is part of being human, and that many students find A&P hard. And you speak to yourself with warmth instead of contempt.

Some people worry that being kind to themselves will make them lazy. The research points the other way. In a set of experiments with college students, people who were guided to think about a failure with self-compassion were more motivated to improve afterward. In one experiment, students who did poorly on a hard test and then received a short self-compassion message spent more time studying for a second test than students who got a self-esteem boost or no message (Breines & Chen, 2012). These were short lab studies, so do not expect miracles, but the direction is clear: kindness and high standards can go together.

Myth or fact?

"If I am hard on myself, I will try harder next time." Mostly myth. Harsh self-talk tends to make people avoid the thing that hurt them, and avoidance is the opposite of what a low exam score needs. A more useful voice sounds like a good coach: "That stung. A lot of people miss action potential questions. What exactly did I miss, and what will I do differently?"

After a disappointing quiz, which inner voice best fits self-compassion?

  1. "It does not matter, the quiz was unfair anyway."
  2. "I am just not a science person."
  3. "That hurt, and that is normal. Let me look at what I missed and plan one change."
  4. "I should stay up all night to punish myself into studying."

Self-compassion is honest about the hurt, remembers that struggle is common, and still looks for the next step. It is not excuses and it is not punishment.

Sleep: the study tool you cannot skip

Sleep is not time taken away from studying. It is part of studying. While you sleep, your brain strengthens and organizes what you learned during the day, which Chapter 8 covers in more detail. A tired brain also struggles to pay attention, which means you learn less from every hour you are awake.

Sleep experts recommend that adults get seven or more hours of sleep a night on a regular basis (Watson et al., 2015). Many college students get less, especially those who work, commute, or care for children. If that is you, the goal is not perfection. The goal is to protect as much sleep as your real life allows, and to stop trading it away for extra studying that will not stick.

Sleep basics that actually help

  • Keep a steady wake-up time, even on days off, as much as you can. Your body clock likes regularity.
  • Get daylight in the morning and dim the lights in the evening.
  • Give your brain a buffer. Stop intense studying, scrolling or gaming a little before bed. Easy flashcard review is fine.
  • Nap short and early. A nap of about 20 minutes in the early afternoon can help. Long or late naps make night sleep harder.
  • Watch the caffeine clock (more on that below).
  • Skip the all-nighter. Studying all night before an exam trades the sleep that locks in memory for a few hours of tired, low-quality review.

If you work nights or rotating shifts, try to keep one "anchor" block of sleep at the same time on most days, use blackout curtains or an eye mask, and plan your hardest studying for the hours when you are most alert, not the hours that happen to be free.

A&P in action

Your sleep schedule is pure BIO 243 nervous system. Some cells in your retina send signals not to the brain's vision areas but to a small cluster of neurons in the hypothalamus called the suprachiasmatic nucleus. It acts as your body's master clock and helps set your daily rhythm of sleep and wakefulness. That is why morning light helps you wake up and bright screens at midnight can push your clock later. You will meet the retina's pathways in chapter 14.

Caffeine and energy drinks, honestly

Caffeine works. It blocks receptors for adenosine, a chemical that builds up while you are awake and makes you feel sleepy. With those receptors blocked, you feel more alert. Used well, a cup of coffee before a morning lecture is a perfectly reasonable tool.

The trouble is the timing and the amount. Caffeine stays in your body for hours. On average, about half of it is still there roughly five hours after you take it, and people vary a great deal; some clear it much faster, some much slower. In one sleep-lab study, a large dose of caffeine taken even six hours before bedtime cut total sleep by more than an hour (Drake et al., 2013). Many people in that situation do not notice that their sleep got worse. They just feel more tired the next day and reach for more caffeine.

A few honest guidelines:

  • The U.S. Food and Drug Administration says that about 400 mg a day is an amount not generally linked to dangerous effects in healthy adults. People who are pregnant, have heart conditions, take certain medicines, or are sensitive to caffeine may need much less; ask a health professional.
  • Read the label. Energy drinks range widely, and some cans contain as much caffeine as several cups of coffee. Pre-workout powders and caffeine pills can be stronger still.
  • Set a caffeine cutoff, such as early afternoon, and see how your sleep changes over a week or two.
  • Caffeine does not replace sleep. It hides sleepiness for a while. It does not give you the memory benefits of sleep.

Rest on purpose

Rest is not the same as quitting. A plan with no rest in it is a plan that breaks around week 9, usually at the worst moment. Build rest into your week the same way you build in study blocks: one evening, or one half day, that belongs to you, your family, your faith, your friends, or nothing at all. Inside study sessions, take real breaks between blocks. Stand up, get water, look at something far away. A break spent scrolling often leaves you more drained than when you started.

Watch for the early signs that you are running on empty: rereading the same paragraph four times, getting irritated at small things, dreading the textbook more than usual. Those are signals to rest or change what you are doing, not proof that you are lazy. Pushing harder through exhaustion feels productive, but it usually produces very little learning.

Moving your body

Physical activity is one of the best supported things you can do for both body and mood. Federal guidelines for adults recommend at least 150 minutes a week of moderate activity, like brisk walking, and they list better sleep and reduced anxiety among the benefits (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, 2018). The guidelines also make a point that matters for busy students: some activity is better than none, and short bouts count.

You do not need a gym membership. Walk to the far parking lot. Take a ten-minute walk between study blocks and say your flashcards out loud as you go. Stretch during a break. Movement is also a good reset when you have been staring at the brachial plexus for too long.

The stress curve

Not all stress is bad. A little pressure helps you focus: the exam next week is what gets you to open the book. Too little pressure and you drift. Too much, and your thinking narrows, you sleep badly, and facts you know seem to vanish.

This idea is often drawn as an upside-down U and linked to a 1908 study by Yerkes and Dodson. Be careful with it. The original study was on mice learning a simple task, and modern researchers treat the curve as a loose rule of thumb rather than a precise law. Still, as a way to think about your week, it is useful: the goal is not zero stress, it is stress you can work with.

What helps when stress runs high

  • Slow your breathing. Breathe in gently, then breathe out a little longer than you breathed in, for a minute or two.
  • Write the worry down, then write one next step beside it. Worries feel bigger when they live only in your head.
  • Shrink the task. "Learn the cranial nerves" is overwhelming. "Learn I through IV tonight" is not.
  • Move, even for ten minutes.
  • Talk to someone: a friend, a family member, your SI Leader, your instructor.

A&P in action

The racing heart and sweaty palms before an exam are your sympathetic nervous system, which you will study in chapter 15. It raises heart rate, widens the airways, dilates the pupils and slows digestion, getting your body ready for action. That is why a nervous stomach and a pounding heart often come together. Knowing the physiology can take some of the fear out of it: this is your body trying to help, a little too enthusiastically. Chapter 9 of this book shows how to use that idea on exam day.

  1. Pause before you decide anything big. Give the feeling a day. Choices made in the first hour after a bad grade are rarely your best choices.
  2. Look at the facts. What exactly happened? Chapter 10's exam wrapper helps you sort misses into clear categories.
  3. Tell the right people early. If life got in the way, let your instructor know as soon as you can. The Dean of Students Office can help with documentation for absences. Waiting usually shrinks your options.

Chapter 13

Help Is a Strategy: Using Campus Support

Strong students ask early, and they ask well

The students who do best in A&P are not the ones who never need help. They are the ones who go get it early, with a specific question, from the right place. This chapter shows you where help lives at USC Upstate and exactly how to ask for it.

Why strong students ask for help early

Many students carry a quiet belief: "If I were really smart enough for this, I would not need help." That belief costs a lot of points in BIO 243.

Think about how skilled people actually work. A nurse who is unsure about a medication dose asks a pharmacist. A surgeon talks through a hard case with colleagues. Asking is not a sign of weakness in health care. It is a safety habit. College works the same way.

Researchers who study help-seeking separate two kinds. Adaptive help-seeking is asking for the help you need to learn something yourself: a hint, an explanation, a better way to study. The other kind is avoiding help altogether, often because asking feels embarrassing, or asking only for answers so the work gets done. Studies of students in classrooms have found that the students who would gain the most from help are often the ones most likely to avoid it, frequently because they worry about looking incapable in front of others (Ryan et al., 2001). Knowing that pattern exists can help you notice it in yourself and push past it.

Timing matters as much as asking. In BIO 243, the topics build on each other: chemistry supports cells, cells support tissues, tissues support everything after. And as the syllabus warns, it is very hard to recover from a low test grade. Help in week 3 can change your Exam 1 score. Help in week 12 can only change what is left.

Myth or fact?

"Going to tutoring or SI is for students who are failing." Myth. Many students who use SI and tutoring are already doing fine and want to do better. Help is a study strategy, like flashcards or practice questions, not an emergency service. Going early, while things are still going well, is when it does the most good.

It also helps to remember that struggling in A&P is common and usually temporary. Research on belonging found that when first-year college students heard that early struggles are normal and tend to improve, some groups of students went on to earn better grades over time (Walton & Cohen, 2011). Chapter 1 explores that idea. Here is the practical version: struggling does not mean you are in the wrong place. It means it is time to use your support.

Student hours: your instructor wants to see you

Student hours (often called office hours) are times your instructor sets aside to meet with students. Many students never go, often because they think they need a big problem first, or they feel they would be bothering the instructor. You are not bothering anyone. Student hours exist for you.

Check your syllabus or Blackboard for your instructor's student hours and how they work (in person, online, or both). If you work or have class during those times, email to ask whether another time is possible. Many instructors will try to find one.

Go with a specific question

The most useful visits start with something specific. "I don't get the nervous system" is hard to answer in fifteen minutes. "I understand the dorsal root carries sensory information into the spinal cord, but I mix up why the ventral root is motor" is a question your instructor can help you untangle in five.

Bring:

  • Your notes or Reading Guide page for the topic, so your instructor can see how you are studying.
  • The exact OpenStax figure or section you are stuck on.
  • Your attempt: a flow chart, an explanation you wrote, a practice question you missed and why you chose your answer.
  • After an exam: your exam wrapper (Chapter 10), so you can talk about patterns, not just single questions.

A&P in action

Compare two openings at student hours. Opening one: "I'm lost on muscles." Opening two: "I drew the cross-bridge cycle from memory. I'm not sure whether ATP binding makes the myosin head attach or let go. Can you check my drawing?" The second one shows your thinking, so your instructor can find the exact gap. (The answer, from OpenStax section 10.3: ATP binding makes the myosin head let go of actin.) Visits that start like this are usually quick, friendly and very productive.

Smart AI move

Not sure how to put your confusion into words? Try a prompt like: "I am confused about saltatory conduction in myelinated axons. Supplemental Instruction and peer tutoring

Supplemental Instruction (SI)

SI Leaders are students who did well in the course and are trained to help others. They attend the class and hold regular weekly sessions focused on the hardest content. An SI session is not a second lecture. It is guided practice: working problems, comparing notes, drawing processes, and quizzing each other with someone who knows the material and remembers what it felt like to learn it.

How well does SI work? A large review of research on SI and similar peer-led study sessions found that students who attended tended to earn higher grades and were less likely to fail or withdraw (Dawson et al., 2014). The honest caveat is that most of those studies could not randomly assign students to attend, and students who choose to go may be more motivated to begin with. Even so, the pattern across many studies is consistent and encouraging.

To get the most out of SI:

  • Go regularly, starting early in the term, not just before exams.
  • Do the reading first, so you can work instead of just listening.
  • Bring questions from lecture and your Reading Guide.
  • Talk. Explaining an idea out loud to others is one of the best ways to find out what you actually understand.

Peer tutoring through QuadC

USC Upstate offers Peer Tutoring, scheduled through QuadC. Tutoring is more one-on-one or small group than SI, which makes it good for working on your specific trouble spots. Book a session as soon as you see a topic slipping, and book a regular time if you can. As with student hours, bring specific questions and your own attempts. A tutor can help you build skills, but a tutor will not do graded work for you, and should not.

You are confused about how the sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions differ. Which plan uses campus help best?

Study groups that actually work

Not all help comes from an office. A good study group can be one of your best resources, and the BIO 243 syllabus says it plainly: studying together is wonderful. The key word is studying. A group that spends two hours rereading slides together, or chatting with the textbook closed, mostly feels productive. A group that quizzes each other actually is.

A few habits make the difference:

  • Keep it small. Three or four people is plenty.
  • Everyone comes prepared. Each person does the reading and brings two questions they wrote themselves.
  • Take turns teaching. One person explains the stages of an action potential with the book closed; the others check it against OpenStax.
  • Draw on a shared board. Have each person draw a process, like the reflex arc, from memory, then compare drawings.
  • Stop at graded work. Study together, then answer Guided Practice, the Reading Guide, CircleIn posts, quizzes and exams on your own.

If you commute or work and cannot meet in person, an online group works too. A short video call after the kids are asleep, with everyone bringing five flashcards to quiz the others, can do more than an hour of studying alone in front of the same notes.

How to email your instructor

Email is how a lot of college business gets done, and a clear email gets a faster, better answer. Instructors often teach several courses and sections, so help them help you.

Email basics

When help does not click the first time

Sometimes you work up the courage to ask, and it does not go the way you hoped. The SI session moved too fast. Try these moves before you give up on a resource:

  • Ask for a different angle. "Could you show me that with a drawing?" or "Can we try a practice question instead?" Most helpers are glad to switch approaches.
  • Try a different person. Another SI Leader, another tutor, or student hours instead of tutoring. Explanations click differently with different people.
  • Come back better prepared. If a session felt over your head, do the reading first next time and bring your attempt.
  • Follow up on emails. A polite second message after two business days is normal and expected.
  • Tell your advisor if scheduling makes every option impossible. Advisors often know about online sessions or other options you have not heard of.

Also be honest with yourself about one trap: going to help and then only listening. Sitting in an SI session while others answer can feel like progress. Real progress comes when you try a question out loud, get it wrong in a safe place, and fix it. That is uncomfortable for almost everyone at first. The final-exam countdown

The BIO 243 final is mostly cumulative, with some new material from the last chapters, which in a typical term means chapter 14 (the somatic nervous system and special senses) and chapter 15 (the autonomic nervous system). Check your syllabus for the exact coverage. A cumulative exam can feel enormous. It becomes manageable when you break it into a plan and start early enough, usually about two weeks out.

The most important rule for a cumulative final: weakest topics first. It is tempting to start with the chapters you already like, because studying them feels good. But points are hiding in the topics you avoided. Your best guide is your own record: Exams 1, 2 and 3, and the exam wrappers you filled out afterward.

Step 1: Take inventory

List every chapter covered: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 9, 10, 12, 13, 16, plus the new chapters 14 and 15. Give each one a traffic-light color:

  • Red: "I could not explain this to a classmate right now." Missed a lot of these questions on the unit exam.
  • Yellow: "I mostly get it, but the details slip."
  • Green: "I could teach this."

Be honest. A yellow you call green will not get studied.

Try it now

Grab a sheet of paper and write the BIO 243 chapter numbers down the left side. Next to each, write red, yellow or green. Then circle your three reddest topics. Those three are where your first study blocks go this week. The whole exercise takes about two minutes and replaces a lot of "where do I even start" stress.

Step 2: Follow the countdown

A few notes on each stage:

  • Days 14 to 12. Do the inventory, then turn it into a calendar. Put your red topics into specific study blocks with specific tasks: "Chapter 10: draw the cross-bridge cycle from memory, then 15 practice questions."
  • Days 11 to 7. Attack red topics with retrieval, not rereading. Redo old Guided Practice problems and your own flashcards for those chapters. At the same time, keep up with chapters 14 and 15 in class. New material still needs its regular reading, Reading Guide and review.
  • Days 6 to 3. Switch to mixed practice. Shuffle flashcards from every chapter together. Answer practice questions in random order. Mixing feels harder than studying one chapter at a time, and that difficulty is part of why it prepares you for an exam that jumps between topics (Rohrer & Taylor, 2007).
  • Days 2 to 1. Focus on the cards you keep missing, look over your exam wrappers, and get a full night of sleep. Chapter 8 explains why sleep beats an all-nighter.
  • Exam day. Use the strategies from Chapter 9: read the stem, predict the answer, eliminate, and change an answer only when you have a real reason.

Smart AI move

AI is useful for building mixed cumulative practice from your own materials. Try: "Here are my red topics for a cumulative A&P I final: [topics]. Make me a 20-question mixed practice set that jumps between them, with answers at the end." Then check every answer key item in OpenStax before you trust it, and never paste in real quiz or exam questions. The five rules from Chapter 11 apply all the way to the final.

Two weeks before the cumulative final, where should your first study blocks go?

  1. Your favorite chapters, to build confidence
  2. Your weakest topics, based on your unit exams and exam wrappers
  3. Only chapters 14 and 15, since they are new
  4. Rereading all your notes from the beginning, in order

Weakest topics first. Your unit exams and wrappers show where the most points are waiting. New chapters still need their regular study, and rereading is a weak strategy compared with retrieval.

Do not forget lab

Lab is part of your BIO 243 course grade, so it belongs in your countdown too. Check your syllabus and lab schedule for what is left: a final lab exam, a practical, or remaining lab assignments. For anything that asks you to identify structures, use the blank-diagram method from Chapter 7: cover the labels, name every part out loud, then check. Models, OpenStax figures, and Dr. Garg's free BIO 243 study-tools site and virtual labs are all good practice material. Short, frequent sessions work better than one long one, the same as for lecture.

When the final collides with everything else

The last two weeks of a semester are crowded. You may have finals in three or four courses, a work schedule that did not get the memo, and family plans. A plan that only looks at BIO 243 will break. So build your countdown across all your courses at once.

  • Put every final on one calendar, with how much each one is worth and how ready you feel.
  • Give each course its own blocks, and give the most blocks to the exams that are both heavily weighted and red.
  • Talk to your employer early. Ask now for lighter shifts during exam week, not the day before.
  • Tell the people you live with when your exams are and when you need quiet time. Most families are glad to help when they know what is coming.
  • Keep sleep in the plan. Exam weeks are when students cut sleep first, and it is the cut that hurts the most.

If something serious happens during finals, such as an illness or a family emergency, contact your instructor right away and reach out to the Dean of Students Office. Waiting until after the exam usually leaves fewer options.

The final rewards connections

A cumulative exam is not just a stack of separate chapter quizzes stapled together. Many of the best questions connect chapters. A question about the neuromuscular junction needs chapter 12 (how neurons signal) and chapter 10 (how muscle fibers respond). A question about the resting membrane potential needs chapter 2 (ions and charge) and chapter 3 (the cell membrane and the sodium-potassium pump).

A&P in action

Follow one signal through the whole course. A motor neuron fires an action potential (chapter 12), which depends on sodium and potassium ions (chapter 2) moving across the cell membrane (chapter 3). At the neuromuscular junction, the neuron releases acetylcholine, and the muscle fiber fires its own action potential. Calcium is released inside the fiber, and the sliding filament process shortens the sarcomeres (chapter 10). The muscle pulls on a bone (chapter 6) across a joint (chapter 9). If you can tell that story start to finish, you can answer questions from five chapters at once.

Five days before the final, you shuffle flashcards from chapters 2, 6, 10 and 15 together. Why is this a good move?

  1. It feels easier than studying one chapter at a time
  2. It lets you skip the chapters you are weakest in
  3. Mixed practice makes you decide which idea each question is about, like a cumulative exam does
  4. It is the same as rereading, just faster

Interleaving feels harder, but it trains you to recognize which concept a question is testing, which is exactly what a cumulative exam demands.

Carrying your system into BIO 244 and beyond

For most of you, BIO 243 is a beginning, not an end. Next may be BIOL U244, A&P II, along with other prerequisites for nursing or another health program. Courses like microbiology, chemistry, nutrition or statistics are common, depending on your program. Check the catalog and talk with your advisor about your exact path.

A&P II courses usually move through more body systems, such as the endocrine, cardiovascular, lymphatic and immune, respiratory, digestive, urinary and reproductive systems. Much of that builds directly on what you just learned.

Keep your materials

Do not throw away your notes, flashcards and flow charts after the final. Students who move straight into A&P II often find that the first weeks lean on chapters 1, 2, 4, 12 and 15. A short review over the break, even fifteen minutes a few times a week, uses spacing to keep that foundation from fading (Cepeda et al., 2006). Your CircleIn flashcards and your own decks are already built. Use them.

A light plan for the break

You have earned real rest after the final, so take it. After a week or two, a very small maintenance plan can keep BIO 243 alive without eating your break:

  • Two or three times a week, fifteen minutes: shuffle your old flashcards and do one short round. Keep the ones you miss in a separate pile.
  • Once a week: draw one process from memory, such as the action potential, the cross-bridge cycle or a reflex arc, then check it against OpenStax.
  • Before the new semester starts: skim the first chapters your next course will cover, and reread your one-page "my system" plan.

That is less than an hour a week. It turns your BIO 243 knowledge from something you once knew into something you still know, which is exactly what A&P II will expect on day one. If you like using AI to quiz yourself over the break, the same rules apply as during the semester: ask it to quiz you, check every answer in OpenStax, and keep anything personal out of it.

Many health programs test A&P again

Many nursing programs ask applicants to take an entrance exam, such as the ATI TEAS, which includes a science section with anatomy and physiology. Requirements vary a lot between programs, so check each program you are applying to. Either way, what you learned in BIO 243 is not a box you check once. It shows up again in later courses, on entrance exams, and in clinical work, where knowing why the heart rate rises when the sympathetic system is active is part of caring for real patients.

Myth or fact?

"Once the final is over, I can let A&P I go." Myth. Memory fades quickly without review, as you learned in Chapter 5. A&P II, nursing courses and entrance exams all build on this material. The good news is that relearning something you once knew well is usually much faster than learning it the first time, especially if you kept your notes and cards.

New course, new syllabus

Every new course is a fresh start with its own rules. In week 1 of BIO 244, or any course, do what you learned here: read the syllabus closely, find the exam schedule, set up your weekly template, find the SI sessions and student hours, and check what that instructor says about AI. Rule 5 from Chapter 11 travels with you: every class is different, so follow that syllabus, and when in doubt, ask.

If this semester did not go the way you hoped

Not everyone reaches the end of BIO 243 with the grade they wanted. Some students will need to retake the course. If that is you, please hear this clearly: it happens to many people who go on to become excellent nurses, therapists and technologists. A&P is often the first college course that demands a real study system, and many students only build that system the second time through.

If you are worried about your final grade, do three things before the semester ends:

  1. Talk with your instructor about where you stand and what the final can still change. Ask in general terms; your instructor can explain how the grade is calculated for your situation.
  2. Talk with your academic advisor about what a retake would mean for your program, your timeline and your next semester's schedule.
  3. Talk with Financial Aid if a retake or a change in your course load could affect your aid.

Then use everything in this chapter anyway. The reflection questions below matter even more for a second attempt. A student who retakes A&P with a written plan, a weekly SI habit and honest notes on what went wrong is in a very different position from the student who simply signs up again. You will not be starting from zero. You will be starting with experience.

Building your study identity

By now you know that "smart" is not a fixed label handed out at birth. Doing well in A&P depends heavily on what you do: how you plan, how you study, how you respond when things go wrong. Researchers call students who manage this cycle well self-regulated learners. They plan before they study, watch how it is going while they study, and reflect afterward so they can adjust (Zimmerman, 2002). That is a description of skills, and skills can be learned.

A related idea is metacognition: thinking about your own thinking. A biology education article describes simple questions students can ask at each stage (Tanner, 2012). Before: "What do I already know about this, and what is my plan?" During: "Do I actually understand this, or does it just look familiar?" After: "What worked, and what will I change next time?"

What about growth mindset, the idea that believing your abilities can grow helps you succeed? It is a popular idea, and the research is more modest than many articles suggest. Large reviews found that, on average, mindset was only weakly related to grades and that mindset programs had small effects overall (Sisk et al., 2018). A large national study of high school students found that a short online mindset program did produce small improvements in grades, mainly for lower-achieving students (Yeager et al., 2019). The fair takeaway: believing you can improve is helpful, but belief alone does not do the work. What changes your A&P grade is what you do with that belief: the study blocks, the flashcards, the student hours visit.

So instead of asking "Am I a science person?", try describing yourself by your habits: "I am someone who reads before class." "I am someone who tests myself instead of rereading." "I am someone who goes to SI." Those are identities you can choose, and you prove them every week by doing them.

Which statement best matches what research says about growth mindset?

  1. Believing you can improve may help a little, but study habits and practice do most of the work
  2. A growth mindset alone will raise your A&P grade a full letter
  3. Mindset has been proven to have no effect at all
  4. Only students with a growth mindset can pass A&P

Large reviews and a national study found small average effects, larger for some students. Belief helps most when it leads to better actions, such as retrieval practice and seeking help.

Reflect before you move on

Reflection is the "adjust" step of the cycle, done on the whole semester. Before the term ends, spend fifteen minutes on these questions. Write your answers down; thinking them is not the same.

  1. Which study strategy made the biggest difference for me in BIO 243?
  2. Which strategy did I keep meaning to use but did not? What got in the way?
  3. When did I fall behind, and what was happening in my life at that time?
  4. Which kind of help (SI, tutoring, student hours, CircleIn, a study group) helped most?
  5. How did I use AI, and did it help me learn or just help me feel finished?
  6. What is one thing I will do differently in my next science course, starting in week 1?

Your one-page "my system" plan

The last step is to put your answers into a single page you can carry forward. Keep it short. A plan you can read in one minute is a plan you will actually use. Figure 14.4 shows one way to lay it out, with an example line in each box.

Fill in each box with specifics:

  • My week: when your best study hours are, and how many blocks you need for a science course.
  • My reading: your routine for textbook chapters.
  • My notes: how and when you rewrite and reconcile.
  • My practice: the retrieval tools you will use every week.
  • My AI rules: the five rules, plus anything a new syllabus adds.
  • My help team: the people and places you will use, and how often.
  • Warning signs: your early signals that things are slipping, and your first response. Include Counseling Services (864.503.5195) and 988 here, in case the warning signs are about more than school.
  • My why: one sentence about why this matters to you.

Save it where you will see it: a photo on your phone, the first page of your next notebook, or a pinned note. Then, in week 1 of your next course, read it before you do anything else.

One last word

A&P I is one of the hardest courses on the path to a health career. It asks you to learn a new language, understand invisible processes, and manage your time while life keeps happening. If you made it here, through the action potentials and the cranial nerves and three unit exams, you have already shown the most important thing: you can learn hard material when you have a plan and keep adjusting it.

Think about what that means for the future. The next hard course, the nursing entrance exam, the first clinical rotation where a patient's chart is full of terms you once found strange: each of those will feel like week 1 of A&P for a little while. You now know what to do with that feeling. Make a plan, start small, test yourself, ask for help early, check your sources, protect your sleep, and adjust when something is not working.

Take that with you. The content will change. The system stays yours.

Key terms

Cumulative final
An exam that covers material from the whole course, in BIO 243 with some new material from the last chapters.
Weakest topics first
The rule of starting final-exam review with the chapters you know least well, based on your exams and wrappers.
Traffic-light inventory
Rating every chapter red, yellow or green to decide where review time should go.
Countdown plan
A day-by-day review schedule that starts about two weeks before an exam.
Mixed practice (interleaving)
Practicing questions from different topics in a shuffled order instead of one topic at a time.
Study system
Your personal set of planning, reading, note-taking, practice and help-seeking habits.
Self-regulated learner
A student who plans, monitors and reflects on their own learning and adjusts as needed.
Metacognition
Thinking about your own thinking, such as checking whether you truly understand something.
Growth mindset
The belief that abilities can improve with effort and good strategies; research shows modest, not dramatic, effects.
Reflection
Looking back on what worked and what did not, in writing, to plan changes.
My system plan
A one-page summary of your study habits that you carry into your next course.
Neuromuscular junction
The synapse where a motor neuron releases acetylcholine to trigger an action potential in a skeletal muscle fiber.
