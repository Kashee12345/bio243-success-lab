Plan Backward: Exam Prep Week by Week

A timeline that starts ten days out and ends with a good night's sleep

Good exam scores in BIO 243 are rarely built in the last two nights. They are built over the ten days before the exam, one short session at a time. This chapter shows you how to start from exam day, count backward, and lay out a plan you can actually follow, even with a job, a commute or a family.

Why plan backward?

Most students plan forward. They study what feels urgent today and hope it adds up by exam day. The trouble is that "urgent" usually means "due tomorrow," so the exam keeps sliding to the bottom of the list until it suddenly is tomorrow. Planning backward flips that. You start with the exam, decide what has to be true by then, and work back to what you need to do today.

It matters a lot in BIO 243 because of how the course is built. There are three unit exams plus a cumulative final, and the syllabus is honest about it: it is very hard to recover from a low test grade, so it pays to start strong. In a typical term, Exam 1 lands around week 5 and covers Chapters 1 to 4. Exam 2 lands around week 8 and covers Chapters 5, 6, 9 and 10. Exam 3 comes around week 13 and covers Chapters 12, 13 and 16. The final comes after week 15. Check your syllabus and Blackboard for the exact timing in your section.

That is why this chapter is tied to weeks 4, 8 and 13. You will want to come back to it before each exam. The first time, use it to build your plan. The second and third times, you will be adjusting a plan you already know.

Planning backward also uses the strategies from Chapter 6 in the right shape. Ten days gives you room for spacing, so every topic gets more than one visit. It gives you time for retrieval practice and a full practice test, with days left over to fix what the practice test reveals. A two-night cram has room for none of that. In a large online study of spacing, Nicholas Cepeda and his colleagues (2008) found that the best gap between study sessions grew as the test got farther away. For a test about a week off, sessions spaced roughly a day apart worked far better than sessions minutes apart. Ten days of short sessions gives you exactly that kind of gap.

The ten-day timeline

Start your exam prep about 7 to 10 days before the exam. Ten is ideal. Seven still works if you are organized. Fewer than that, and you are mostly sorting out what you can skip. Figure 8.1 shows the four phases.

Days 10 to 8: map it

This phase is short, maybe one or two study blocks. You are not learning yet. You are making a map.

  • List what the exam covers. Write every chapter and, under each, the main topics or learning objectives your instructor has given. If there is a study guide or objectives list in Blackboard, start there.
  • Rate each topic. Use a traffic light. Green: I could explain this to a classmate right now without notes. Yellow: I sort of know it, but I would hesitate. Red: I am lost, or I have not studied it yet. Be honest. Nobody sees this list but you.
  • Gather your materials. Your rewritten notes, your flashcards, your blank diagrams, the OpenStax chapters, and the Reading Guide. Check how much of the Reading Guide you have finished.
  • Look at your real calendar. When are your work shifts, classes, family duties and commutes over the next ten days? Find your study blocks first, then assign topics to them. Chapter 2, Your Real Week, has more on fitting study around real life.

Days 7 to 4: learn and fill gaps

This is the longest phase and the heart of your prep. Each block focuses on one chapter or one big topic, starting with your red topics while you have the most time and energy. Use the strategies from Chapters 6 and 7: blank-page recall, flashcards in both directions, blank diagrams, and processes written as stories with "because" links.

The key move is to open every block with about ten minutes of mixed review: quick recall on topics from earlier blocks and earlier chapters, shuffled together. That keeps older material from fading while you work on new material, and it gives you the interleaving that Chapter 6 described.

Days 3 to 2: test yourself

Now take a full practice test, closed book, timed, covering every chapter on the exam. Then spend the next day working on what it showed you. Do not skip this phase. It is the one most likely to catch the gap that would have cost you points.

Day 1: wind down

The day before the exam is for light, targeted review, finishing and submitting the Reading Guide, getting your things ready, and sleeping. It is not the day to learn a whole chapter for the first time. The rest of this chapter covers each of these pieces in more detail.

Try it now

Take two minutes and rate the topics for your next exam. If it is Exam 1, your list might include: anatomical terms and body regions, homeostasis and feedback loops, the four tissue types and the types of epithelium, chemical bonds and pH, the parts of the cell, membrane transport, and DNA replication, mitosis and protein synthesis. Write G, Y or R next to each one. Count your reds. That number tells you where your first study blocks go.

Dividing up the chapters

Once you know your reds, yellows and greens, give each topic a share of your study blocks. Two things decide the share: how weak you are on the topic, and how big a part of the exam it is likely to be. A long, process-heavy chapter you rated red needs several blocks. A short chapter you rated green may need only mixed review.

Here is how that might look for Exam 2. Imagine you rated Chapter 10, muscle tissue, red, because the sliding filament model and excitation-contraction coupling still feel shaky. You rated Chapter 6, bone tissue, and Chapter 9, joints, yellow. And you rated Chapter 5, the integumentary system, green, because you studied it well a few weeks ago. Figure 8.2 shows one way to spread those topics across six study days, with one hour each day.

Your own plan will look different. Maybe you have 30 minutes on work days and two hours on your day off. Maybe your red topic is joints instead of muscle. The pattern is what matters: weakest first, mixed review at the start of each block, and a practice test with time left to act on it.

A&P in action

What does "fill gaps" look like for a red topic like Chapter 10? On 7 days out, the plan might be: draw a sarcomere from memory, relaxed and contracted, and label the Z discs, M line, A band, I band and H zone. Check it against OpenStax. Then write three "what happens to" questions: what happens to the I band during contraction (it shortens), to the A band (it stays the same length), and to the distance between Z discs (it shrinks). On 6 days out, write the excitation-contraction story from Chapter 7 with "so" between steps, from acetylcholine release to calcium binding troponin to the power stroke. Then explain it out loud, start to finish, without notes. When you can do that twice on different days, Chapter 10 moves from red to yellow.

Practice tests: your most important study session

A practice test is retrieval practice in the exact form of the real thing. It does two jobs. It strengthens what you know, and it shows you clearly what you do not know while you still have time to fix it. A large meta-analysis by Olusola Adesope and his colleagues (2017) combined the results of many studies and found that practice tests generally led to better learning than restudying or other comparison activities, across many kinds of material and test formats.

How to build one

  • Collect questions from the OpenStax Review Questions at the end of each chapter, the practice items on Dr. Garg's BIO 243 study-tools site, the questions you have written yourself, and good CircleIn questions from classmates.
  • Mix them. Shuffle questions from all the exam chapters together. The real exam will not be sorted by chapter.
  • Use the format of the exam. BIO 243 exams are multiple choice, so most of your practice should be too. Add a few "explain this process" questions for the big processes, because explaining is the deepest test of all.
  • Make it real. Closed book, no phone, timed. Sit at a table, not on your bed.

How to use the results

Grade it honestly, then make an error log: for each miss, write the topic and a short reason. "Mixed up osteoblast and osteoclast." "Did not know which joints are amphiarthroses." "Read too fast and missed the word NOT." Your error log becomes the plan for the next day. Chapter 10, After the Exam, uses the same kind of sorting on the real exam afterward.

Why does the ten-day plan put the practice test 2 to 3 days before the exam instead of the night before?

  1. Practice tests are only useful when you are well rested
  2. So there is still time to study the topics the practice test shows you missed
  3. Because the Reading Guide is due the same night
  4. Practice tests lower your score if taken too close to the exam

A practice test is most valuable as a map of your gaps. Taking it a few days early leaves time to act on what it reveals, instead of just discovering the gaps the night before.

The Reading Guide deadline

The OpenStax Reading Guide by M. Storm is a fillable guide in Blackboard. Completing it before an exam can earn extra credit, and because its answers come straight from the textbook, its content also shows up on exams. That makes it two things at once: a bit of extra credit and a ready-made study tool.

For exam planning, the key fact is the deadline. The Reading Guide for a unit is usually due the night before the exam. Check Blackboard for the exact deadline in your section. Two warnings come with that deadline.

  • Do not save it all for the last night. If the whole Reading Guide is waiting for you on Day 1, it will eat the evening you need for light review and sleep. Fill it in as you read each chapter, then use Day 1 only to finish the last bits and submit.
  • Do it yourself. The Reading Guide is graded work. The BIO 243 AI rules are clear: no AI on quizzes, exams or graded work, and that includes the Reading Guide. Fill it in from the textbook, in your own words where the guide asks for explanation. That is also how it turns into learning.

Once it is submitted, the completed guide is a great review sheet. On Day 1 or exam morning, cover your answers and quiz yourself from the questions.

The day before

Day 1 should feel calm, not frantic. If your plan worked, you have already done the heavy lifting. Here is what the day before can look like:

  • Short, targeted review. Go through your error log and your Box 1 flashcards. Redraw the two or three diagrams you have had the most trouble with. Explain one big process out loud. Keep it to one or two short blocks.
  • No brand-new chapters. If something is still red on Day 1, learn its main ideas, not every detail, and accept that this exam may not be perfect on that topic. Then make a note to start earlier next time.
  • Finish and submit the Reading Guide.
  • Handle logistics. Confirm when and how the exam is given in your section. If it is online in Blackboard, make sure your device is charged and your internet is reliable, and read any instructions about the exam ahead of time. If it is on campus, plan your commute with extra time for traffic and parking.
  • Clear the morning. If you can, arrange child care, trade a shift, or ask for help with family duties well before Day 1, so the morning is yours.

The night before: sleep beats the all-nighter

It is tempting to stay up late "just to get through one more chapter." For most students, most of the time, that trade loses. Sleep is not wasted study time. It is part of how memory works.

Researchers who study sleep and memory, such as Matthew Walker and Robert Stickgold (2006) and Susanne Diekelmann and Jan Born (2010), have reviewed a large body of evidence that sleep after learning helps consolidate new memories, meaning it helps stabilize them and connect them with what you already know. Sleep loss also seems to hurt the next day's learning. In one study by Seung-Schik Yoo and colleagues (2007), people who had been kept awake for a night were noticeably worse at forming new memories the next day than people who had slept normally. An exam asks your brain to retrieve and reason under pressure, and a tired brain does both less well.

There is an important nuance. A study by Kana Okano and colleagues (2019) tracked the sleep of students in a large college chemistry course with wearable devices. Students who slept more, slept better, and kept a more consistent sleep schedule over the weeks of the course tended to earn better grades. Sleep in just the night or two right before a test was not, by itself, strongly linked to scores. The study was correlational, so it cannot prove sleep caused the grades. But it fits a sensible message: one good night will not save a unit you did not study, and steady sleep all along gives you the best chance. The ten-day plan helps you do both, because you are not trying to cram the unit into the last night.

Sleep experts generally recommend that adults get 7 or more hours a night on a regular basis. If you work late shifts or care for young children, that may not always be possible. Protect what you can. Even moving your bedtime 30 minutes earlier the night before an exam is better than pulling it later.

Myth or fact?

"An all-nighter shows I'm serious, and I'll remember more because it's fresh."

Myth. Staying up all night does not show dedication to anyone, and it usually costs more than it gains. You lose the sleep that helps lock in what you studied, and you walk into the exam with a slower, foggier brain. Material crammed in the middle of the night also tends to fade quickly, which hurts again on the cumulative final. If you find yourself facing an all-nighter, it is a sign to start earlier next time, not to push harder tonight. Chapter 12 covers sleep and stress in more depth.

Picture this

Imagine a student named Marcus, a fictional pre-nursing student with a three-year-old and a weekend job. Before Exam 1, Marcus found out the exam date in week 4 and panicked, because his only free time was after his son went to bed. For Exam 2, he planned backward. Ten days out, he rated his topics and put Chapter 10 at the top. On work days, he did one 40-minute block after bedtime. On his day off, he did two. Three days out, he took a practice test at the kitchen table while his partner had their son at the park. The night before, he submitted his Reading Guide by 9, looked at his toughest flashcards for five minutes, and went to bed. The plan was not perfect. He missed one block when his son was sick. But because the plan had slack in it, one missed block did not sink the whole thing.

What to eat and what to bring

You do not need a special "brain food" diet before an exam. You need to not be hungry, thirsty or jittery.

  • Eat a normal meal you are used to. Exam morning is not the day to try something new. A breakfast with some protein and fiber, like eggs and toast, yogurt and fruit, or oatmeal with peanut butter, tends to keep you going longer than a sugary snack alone.
  • Drink water. Bring a bottle if your exam setting allows it.
  • Keep caffeine normal. If you usually have one coffee, have one coffee. Doubling it or adding an energy drink can make you jittery, raise your heart rate, and add to anxious feelings. You will learn in Chapter 15 of OpenStax that caffeine and stress both push your body toward a sympathetic, fight-or-flight state, and you do not need extra of that on exam day.
  • Bring what the exam requires. For an in-person exam that may mean your student ID and pencils. For an online exam in Blackboard, it means a charged device, a reliable connection, and a quiet space. Follow your instructor's instructions exactly.
  • Leave everything else out of reach. The syllabus is clear that using any outside source or device during a quiz or test is cheating. Put your phone away and close other tabs and apps before you start.

It is the night before Exam 2. You have studied steadily for nine days, your Reading Guide is submitted, and it is getting late. What does the research on sleep and memory suggest you do?

  1. Do a short review of your toughest cards, then go to sleep
  2. Stay up to reread all four chapters one more time
  3. Drink an energy drink so you can study until the morning
  4. Skip sleep, since one night does not matter

Sleep after learning helps consolidate memories, and sleep loss makes it harder to think clearly the next day. After nine days of steady work, a short targeted review followed by a full night of sleep protects what you have already learned.

Using the Exam Planner tool

This Success Lab includes an Exam Planner tool that does much of this chapter's math for you. It helps you lay out the days before the exam you are preparing for, using the four phases from Figure 8.1. As you fill it in, follow the same rules as this chapter: put your red topics first, place mixed review at the start of each block, and save the last days for a practice test and fixing misses. Use it once before each exam. Print the result or keep it on your phone, and check items off as you go.

A plan on paper is not a promise you must keep perfectly. Life happens: a sick child, an extra shift, a car that will not start. When a day falls apart, do not throw out the plan. Move the missed block to the next open slot, shrink the green topics, and keep the practice test. That is the point of starting ten days out. It gives your plan room to bend without breaking.

Smart AI move

AI can help you build a study schedule or practice questions, as long as you keep it private and check the content. Try: "I have an anatomy exam in ten days on bone tissue, joints, muscle tissue and the integumentary system. Muscle tissue is my weakest topic. I can study about one hour on weekdays and two hours on weekends. Make a day-by-day plan with mixed review at the start of each session and a practice test three days before the exam." Notice what the prompt leaves out: your name, your student ID, your grades and your school details. You can also ask, "Write ten multiple-choice questions on synovial joint types for a first-year A&P student, with answers at the end." Then check every answer against OpenStax Chapter 9, because AI can be confidently wrong. Never paste actual quiz or exam questions into AI.

Each exam has its own flavor

The same ten-day plan works for every exam, but each one has its own challenges, so adjust your map.

  • Exam 1 (Chapters 1 to 4) is your first exam, so you do not yet know what the questions feel like. It combines a lot of new vocabulary with chemistry and cell processes like protein synthesis and mitosis. Start your map early, around week 4, and pay attention to processes, not just terms.
  • Exam 2 (Chapters 5, 6, 9 and 10) mixes structure-heavy content, like the layers of the skin, bone anatomy and joint types, with one of the big processes of the course, muscle contraction. Balance blank diagrams with process stories.
  • Exam 3 (Chapters 12, 13 and 16) is all nervous system. Action potentials and synapses are process-heavy, while the brain, spinal cord and cranial nerves are structure-heavy, and the neurological exam in Chapter 16 asks you to connect the two. Plan at least one block just for the cranial nerves and how each is tested.
  • The final exam is mostly cumulative, with some new material from the last chapters. It needs a longer countdown that starts with your weakest topics from the whole semester. Chapter 14, Beyond BIO 243, walks you through it.

During the "map it" phase, you rate Chapter 13 (the CNS and cranial nerves) red and Chapter 12 (nervous tissue) green. How should your study blocks for Exam 3 be divided?

  1. Equal time for every chapter, in textbook order
  2. Start with Chapter 12, since it comes first in the book
  3. Skip mixed review so there is more time for Chapter 13
  4. Give Chapter 13 the most blocks and start with it, while keeping some mixed review that includes Chapter 12

The weakest topic gets the most time and comes first, while you are fresh. Mixed review at the start of each block keeps the green chapter from fading, and it gives you interleaving at the same time.

Your five-minute action

  1. Find your next exam in the syllabus and count back ten days. Write that day down as "start exam prep."
  2. List the chapters it covers and rate each main topic red, yellow or green.
  3. Look at your real calendar for those ten days and mark every study block you can protect.
  4. Pick the block that is 2 to 3 days before the exam and label it "practice test."
  5. Check how much of the Reading Guide you have finished, and add the deadline to your phone.

Key terms

Planning backward
Starting from the exam date and working back to decide what needs to happen on each day before it.
Traffic-light rating
Rating each topic green (can explain it), yellow (partly know it) or red (lost or not studied) to decide where study time goes.
Mixed review
A few minutes of shuffled recall on earlier topics at the start of each study block, to keep older material fresh.
Practice test
A timed, closed-book self-test in the format of the real exam, taken early enough to fix the gaps it reveals.
Error log
A running list of the questions you missed, each with its topic and the reason you missed it.
Reading Guide
The fillable OpenStax guide in Blackboard whose answers come from the textbook; completing it before the exam can earn extra credit.
Memory consolidation
The process, helped by sleep, that stabilizes new memories and links them with older knowledge.
All-nighter
Staying awake through the night to study, which costs the sleep that helps memory and clear thinking.
Spacing gap
The time between study sessions on the same topic; for a test days away, gaps of a day or so work better than minutes.
Exam Planner
The Success Lab tool that helps you lay out a ten-day exam prep plan with your own topics and ratings.
